## 0. THE PREREQUISITE: ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

Garments went out printed **OVERTIME**. That is not a print error — a printer printed exactly what they were sent. It is a *supply of truth* error, and every touchpoint below will reproduce it until it is fixed.

Before any of the specifications in this section are used, build **one page** called `OUR TIME — SUPPLIER FACT SHEET`. It is a PDF. It has a version number and a date. It goes to every supplier with every order, attached to the purchase order, not sent once and forgotten. It contains only:

1. **The name, written out:** `OUR TIME` — two words, one space, always uppercase. **Never OVERTIME. Never OURTIME. Never Our Time.** Set this line in 24pt red on the fact sheet. It is the single most expensive mistake the brand has made so far.
2. **The word space rule.** The wordmark is a thin, widely letterspaced uppercase sans. At wide tracking the word space is visually indistinguishable from the letterspace, so it collapses. **Rule: the word space must be set at 3× the letter tracking value, minimum, and must be a hard-coded space in the outlined artwork — never a live font.** All wordmark artwork is supplied to suppliers as **outlined vector only (.ai / .pdf / .eps)**. No live text ever leaves the building. That single rule ends the OURTIME problem permanently, because a supplier cannot re-kern outlines.
3. **The two logo files**, named unambiguously: `OURTIME_MONOGRAM_OT.ai` and `OURTIME_WORDMARK.ai`. Not `logo.ai`, not `logo-final-v2.ai`.
4. **The palette**, as a table of hex, and where it matters, Pantone equivalents obtained by physically matching a printed swatch — not by software conversion. Named list below.
5. **The minimum sizes** (Section 4).
6. **One line on approvals:** *No production run proceeds without a signed physical strike-off / pre-production sample.* Photographs of a screen on a press do not count.

**The palette, as it goes to suppliers:**

| Name | Hex | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | `#0F0F0F` | The brand's black. Every printed black, every digital background. |
| True black | `#000000` | **Photographic backdrop only.** Never ink, never screen. |
| Logo white | `#FAF6F6` | Every white ink, every white garment print. Not `#FFFFFF`. |
| Navy | `#141B2C` / `#141925` | Tee / short bodies |
| Washed black | `#171717` | Garment |
| Washed grey | `#A7A5A4` | Garment |
| Washed denim | `#54647E` | Garment — the most saturated colour the brand owns |
| Off-white | `#DFDDDE` | Garment |
| Cream / sand | `#CFC2B4` | Garment, string, tissue |

**Two combinations are banned outright and must appear on the fact sheet as banned:** `#171717` on `#0F0F0F` (1.07:1 — invisible) and `#A7A5A4` on `#DFDDDE` (1.84:1). These have already caused problems. A print supplier offered "washed black ink on the black tee" will say yes, because it is technically possible. It is not legible.

## 1. GARMENT LABELS

### 1.1 The decision: woven, printed, or heat transfer

| Method | Feel | Cost at 500 | Fails at |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Woven (damask)** | Textured, structural, reads as an established brand | ~**£98.25 / 500 = 20p each** for a 20×60mm straight-cut sew-on label ([Woven Labels UK](https://www.wovenlabelsuk.com/products/essential-custom-woven-labels), verified Sept 2026) | Fine detail — thread cannot hold hairlines |
| **Printed satin/taffeta** | Flat, cheaper, good for long care text | Cheaper per unit than woven, typically 5–15p at 500 *(UNVERIFIED — get quotes)* | Wears/fades faster; can feel budget at the neck |
| **Heat transfer (tagless)** | No label edge, clean interior, very "performance brand" | Low per-unit but needs a heat press and a pretreated, smooth interior surface | Cracks on washed/garment-dyed fabric over time; looks thin on heavyweight cotton |

**The rule for OUR TIME: woven centre-fold loop label at centre-back neck on all seven garment types.**

The convention for oversized heavyweight tees genuinely is split — a lot of athletic and performance labels go tagless for comfort. But the comfort objection is about label edge against skin on thin jersey, and on a 240–320gsm heavyweight body the fabric itself buffers the neckline, which is why woven remains the standard for premium streetwear, heavyweight tees, hoodies and denim ([Rapid Tags](https://www.rapidtags.com/woven-vs-printed-vs-heat-transfer-labels/), [Arcus Apparel](https://www.arcusag.com/clothing-label-basics-woven-vs-printed-vs-heat-transfer/)). More importantly for this brand: one label type across all seven garments is the cheapest possible way to make a 31-colourway range read as **one label** rather than seven separate product decisions. That is exactly the problem OUR TIME has.

**The one exception:** if the blanks are bought pre-labelled and the supplier will not supply them blank-necked, relabelling means unpicking their label, which damages neck tape and costs real money in labour *(UNVERIFIED — typically 50p–£1.00/unit in the UK; get a quote from whoever finishes the garments)*. In that case use heat transfer for that style only, and say so in the range plan rather than discovering it at delivery.

### 1.2 Neck label specification

- **Type:** Woven damask, **centre-fold loop**, sewn into the centre-back neck seam.
- **Size:** 25mm wide × 35mm finished height (so 25 × 70mm flat, folded at centre). *A loop label costs more than a straight-cut label — budget 25–35p each at 500 rather than 20p. (UNVERIFIED)*
- **Artwork:** **OT monogram only.** Not the wordmark. This is the single most important label decision in this section, and here is why: the wordmark is thin and widely tracked, and thread physically cannot hold a hairline. A woven thin sans at 25mm wide will come back as a grey blur, and the word space will close — reproducing the OURTIME failure in a form that cannot be corrected without rebuying 500 labels. The OT monogram is heavy, angular and chunky. It is the mark that weaving is *good* at.
- **Colourways:** Two only. `#FAF6F6` monogram on `#0F0F0F` ground (for all dark garments), and `#0F0F0F` monogram on `#CFC2B4` ground (for cream, off-white, taupe, tan, light blue). Two SKUs of label for a 31-colourway range. Do not make more.
- **Weave:** high-definition damask. Ask for **HD damask, not standard damask** — the OT monogram has sharp angular shear cuts and a coarse weave will round them off. Ask for a physical woven sample (most UK suppliers will produce one) before committing to 500.
- **MOQ:** 500 is standard for HD damask; 50 is available at some suppliers at much worse unit rates.

### 1.3 Care / composition label

This one is legally loaded, so read it carefully.

**What is legally mandatory in the UK: the fibre composition.** Under the Textile Products (Labelling and Fibre Composition) Regulations 2012 (which retain EU Reg. 1007/2011 in UK law), all textile products must carry a label indicating fibre content, and it is the **manufacturer's or importer's** responsibility — which, as the brand owner importing blanks, is the cousin ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1102/introduction/made/data.htm), [Business Companion](https://www.businesscompanion.info/en/quick-guides/goods/labelling-of-textiles)).

The specific rules that will bite:

- The label must be **in English**, and **durable, easily legible, visible and accessible**.
- Only the fibre names listed in Annex I of the Regulation may be used. `COTTON` is legal. `PREMIUM COTTON`, `HEAVYWEIGHT COTTON` or `SOFT-TOUCH COTTON` on the composition line are not.
- **"Pure" may only be used for single-fibre garments.** So `100% PURE COTTON` is fine on a 100% cotton tee and illegal on a 80/20 fleece.
- Multi-fibre products must be listed by percentage in descending order, e.g. `COTTON 80%, POLYESTER 20%`.
- **Decorative matter making up 7% or less of the product is excluded** — which is why a screen print does not need declaring.
- Garments with components of different composition (a hoodie body vs. a rib, a lined jacket) must show **each component separately**.

**Care symbols are NOT legally mandatory in the UK** — but the GINETEX symbols are trademarked, and using them requires a formal licence. In the UK that licence is issued by **UKFT**, is non-transferable, and must be held by each individual company ([UKFT](https://ukft.org/membership/care-label-licence/), [UKFT care labelling](https://ukft.org/worldwide-care-labelling-textiles-apparel/)). *UKFT do not publish the price — it is bundled with membership and quoted on enquiry to Compliance@ukft.org. (UNVERIFIED — budget for it, but it is likely a low-hundreds annual figure for a micro-brand.)*

**Practical route for launch:** get the licence. Every serious brand carries care symbols; retailers and stockists will ask for them; and if a customer wrecks a garment following no instructions at all, the liability sits with the brand under the General Product Safety Regulations. Until the licence is in place, **use words, not symbols** — written care instructions are unlicensed and legal.

**Specification:**
- **Type:** Printed satin or soft-touch nylon, **side-seam**, sewn into the inside left side seam approx. 100mm up from the hem — never at the neck. The neck carries the monogram; the neck must stay clean.
- **Size:** 35mm wide × 60mm, folded once (an "end-fold" or simple mitre-fold).
- **Print:** One colour, `#0F0F0F` on natural/off-white satin. Never reverse-printed (white on black) — small reversed type on satin fills in and becomes unreadable after two washes.
- **Type size:** minimum 6pt. Set in the same thin wide-tracked sans as the wordmark **but at normal tracking**, not the wordmark's wide tracking. The label is the one place the brand's quiet voice is allowed to behave like ordinary type, because legibility is a legal requirement.

**The literal wording block (adapt per garment):**

```
OUR TIME
EST. 2026

100% COTTON

MACHINE WASH COLD 30°
WASH INSIDE OUT WITH LIKE COLOURS
DO NOT BLEACH
TUMBLE DRY LOW
COOL IRON — NOT ON PRINT
DO NOT DRY CLEAN

GARMENT DYED. COLOUR WILL VARY
BETWEEN PIECES AND WILL SOFTEN
WITH WEAR. THIS IS INTENDED.

MADE IN [COUNTRY]
```

Two notes on that block:

- **Country of origin is not legally required in the UK** — but if you state it, it must be true, and "Made in UK" for a garment cut and sewn overseas and only printed here is a misleading claim. If unsure, omit it entirely rather than guess.
- The **garment-dye disclaimer is doing real work.** It is brand voice *and* it is the thing that stops a "the second one is a different colour" refund. Every garment-dyed brand carries a version of it. Ours should be in brand voice, not legalese.

### 1.4 Size label

**Do not buy a separate size label.** Print the size onto the care label as the **top line**, in a size band, and buy one care-label SKU per size rather than per garment. That is seven sizes × one artwork, not 31 SKUs.

```
—————  L  —————
```

Size the character at 12pt minimum, centred, with the two rules either side set to the wordmark's tracking. That small detail — the wordmark's wide tracking appearing as *rules*, not letters — is the brand's typographic signature applied where it costs nothing.

**Size range naming:** use `S / M / L / XL / XXL`, uppercase, no lowercase, no "Small". On an oversized range, also carry a **garment measurement table on the website** (chest pit-to-pit, body length, sleeve) — for an oversized fit, the letter alone is meaningless and it is the single biggest driver of returns.

## 2. SWING TAGS

The swing tag is the brand's only physical print piece that the customer holds and reads. It is also the cheapest thing in this section. Spend the money here.

### 2.1 Specification

| | |
|---|---|
| **Size** | **45 × 90mm portrait.** Not 85 × 55mm. That is the default landscape "business card with a hole in it" size that every UK trade printer offers — `instantprint` list it as their "classic swing and parcel tag" at 85 × 55mm ([instantprint](https://www.instantprint.co.uk/cards-and-invites/swing-tags)) — and it reads as generic on sight. A tall narrow tag reads as considered, costs the same, and gives the wide-tracked wordmark the horizontal room it needs. |
| **Stock** | **450gsm uncoated, natural/recycled**, no lamination. Uncoated is non-negotiable: the whole range is washed, matte and tactile, and a laminated tag will feel like a voucher. For the step up, duplex two 300gsm uncoated sheets to 600gsm with a visible core — that edge is the entire premium signal and it is a ~£30 uplift on a launch run. *(UNVERIFIED — duplex is a specialist job, not available from instant-quote trade printers.)* |
| **Finish** | Print only at launch. **Blind deboss** of the OT monogram is the correct next upgrade — it is colourless, tactile and matte, which is exactly the brand. **Never** foil, never gloss spot UV, never gold. Spot UV Gold and Silver are offered as standard options by UK trade printers and both are wrong for this brand. |
| **Hole** | 4mm, punched **at the top centre**. Trade printers punch 4mm as standard. |
| **String** | **Waxed cotton thread in `#CFC2B4` cream**, hand-tied, ~120mm. Trade printers include 15cm white **nylon** string free — it is plastic, it is bright white against a `#FAF6F6` warm-white brand, and it is the single cheapest thing to get wrong. A 500m spool of waxed cotton is a few pounds. Tie them yourself. **No plastic barbed pins anywhere** — a plastic tagging gun punches a hole through the fabric, which on a premium garment-dyed piece is a downgrade the customer can see. |

### 2.2 What goes on each side

**Face A (front) — the loud voice, nothing else:**

- **OT monogram**, 26mm wide, centred, optically positioned slightly above centre (the monogram is italic/right-leaning; centre it *optically*, not mathematically, or it will look like it is falling over).
- Nothing else. No tagline, no URL, no "EST. 2026". The front of the tag is the brand's one uninterrupted statement.
- **Colour:** `#0F0F0F` on natural stock. On a dark-stock variant, `#FAF6F6` on `#0F0F0F`.

**Face B (reverse) — the quiet voice, and all the information:**

Set as one column, left-aligned, generous leading, in the thin wide-tracked wordmark face:

```
OUR TIME
EST. 2026

[ONE LINE OF COPY — rotate per drop:
 EARN EVERY SECOND. / TIME WON'T WAIT. /
 DISCIPLINE. FOCUS. CONSISTENCY.]

—

57.5878° N  4.2383° W
THE BLACK ISLE, SCOTLAND

—

100% COTTON
SIZE L
GARMENT DYED — COLOUR WILL VARY

[QR]     OURTIME.[TLD]
```

The **coordinates belong on the tag**, not just on the globe tee. That one line is the brand's biggest untapped differentiator (Section 5.1) and it costs nothing to put on every garment. It also turns a generic grind-culture tagline into a claim with a *place* attached, which is what makes it credible.

### 2.3 The QR code

- **Position:** bottom-left of Face B. Never on the front.
- **Size:** **20 × 20mm minimum** printed. Below that, phone cameras start failing at an arm's length in normal indoor light. *(Standard industry guidance; not a formal standard — verify by testing the actual printed tag with three different phones before signing off the run.)*
- **Quiet zone:** 4 modules of clear space on all sides. No artwork, no rule, no string hole inside the quiet zone.
- **Colour:** `#0F0F0F` on the natural stock. **Never** reversed light-on-dark — many older scanners will not read an inverted code.
- **Where it points:** a **per-drop landing page**, not the homepage and not Instagram. Something like `ourtime.[tld]/ledger/[drop-code]` — this is the natural physical entry point into THE LEDGER. The tag is the index card; the page is the file it refers to. Print the URL in plain text underneath it as well, because a meaningful fraction of people will never scan.
- **Use a dynamic/redirecting QR** (a short URL you control that you can repoint later) so that 500 printed tags do not become dead when the site structure changes. Do not use a free QR service that can expire or insert an interstitial ad.

### 2.4 Cost

Verified trade pricing at `instantprint` for 85 × 55mm, 350gsm uncoated, with hole and string: **from £12.89 for 25, up to 2,000 units for £56.34** — approx. **2.8p per tag at 2,000**. 500 units on that spec is not published *(UNVERIFIED — expect roughly £25–35, i.e. 5–7p each)*.

Stepping up to 450gsm uncoated recycled at a non-instant printer at 500 units: *budget **£60–£110, i.e. 12–22p each** (UNVERIFIED — get three quotes, and specify "no lamination" explicitly or they will quote laminated by default).* Blind deboss adds a one-off die charge, *typically £40–80 (UNVERIFIED)*.

**This is a £100 decision on a launch drop. Do not economise here.**

## 3. PACKAGING & THE UNBOXING SEQUENCE

### 3.1 The sequence, defined

The unboxing is a **five-beat sequence**, and each beat introduces exactly one thing. This is written out because it is the order that gets improvised badly when a founder is packing 40 orders at 11pm.

1. **The mailer.** Matte, near-black `#0F0F0F` or natural kraft. One small OT monogram, bottom-right, 25mm. No wordmark, no tagline, no URL. Deliberately quiet — the outside of the parcel is not a billboard, and an unbranded-looking parcel is also the one that does not get stolen from a doorstep.
2. **The seal.** A **45mm circular sticker** closing the folded tissue. `#FAF6F6` on `#0F0F0F`. The artwork: the **Roman-numeral clock face with OT at 12** — the motif the brand already owns, and a circular sticker is the one format it was born for. Breaking the seal is the beat.
3. **The tissue.** Unprinted, acid-free, `#CFC2B4` cream, one sheet, folded around the garment. *Printed tissue (all-over OT tessellation, one colour) is the upgrade — but at launch quantities custom tissue has high MOQs (typically 500–1,000 sheets minimum) and the cream unprinted sheet with a strong seal sticker reads more premium than badly-printed tissue. (UNVERIFIED on MOQ — varies by supplier.)*
4. **The garment**, folded face-up, swing tag visible, monogram reading the right way up.
5. **The card**, sitting on top of the tissue so it is seen before the garment.

### 3.2 The mailer: the recycled vs. compostable decision, argued honestly

This is the decision most likely to be made emotionally. Here is the actual position.

**What compostable costs:** certified compostable mailers (70% PBAT / 5% PLA / 25% cornstarch, certified to EN13432, ASTM D6400, AS4736) run **£6.35 per pack of 50 (12.7p each) at one pack, falling to £4.19 per pack (8.4p each) at 200+ packs** ([SR Mailing](https://www.srmailing.co.uk/products/compostable-mailing-bags), verified Sept 2026). They have a **12–18 month shelf life from production** — which means a founder who buys 2,000 to get the unit rate down and then sells slowly will be posting degrading bags in year two.

**What compostable actually does in the UK:** mostly nothing. Most UK councils do not accept compostable packaging in household food-waste collections; food and garden waste is processed on a roughly six-week cycle while compostable packaging takes far longer, so it contaminates the compost output. DEFRA's network of in-vessel composting and anaerobic digestion facilities that will take compostable packaging separately is limited, and in practice a certified compostable item usually ends up in general waste, landfill or incineration ([WRAP](https://www.wrap.ngo/resources/guide/considerations-compostable-plastic-packaging), [Compost Connect UK](https://www.compostconnect.org/uk/how-to-dispose-of-compostable-packaging/)). WRAP is explicit that compostable packaging is not a solution to littering and that implying it will safely disappear is **factually wrong and a greenwashing risk under CMA Green Claims guidance**.

**What recycled LDPE costs:** plain grey recycled LDPE mailers are **1–2p each** at 500-pack retail *(source: [mrbags.co.uk UK pricing guide](https://mrbags.co.uk/blogs/blog/how-much-do-mailing-bags-cost-uk) — UNVERIFIED, treat as indicative)*; custom-printed 100% recycled poly mailers from a branded supplier will be substantially more *(noissue and similar quote per project — UNVERIFIED)*. A recycled LDPE mailer is genuinely recyclable at supermarket soft-plastic collection points, which most people in the UK can actually reach.

**Paper mailers** sit in between: Priory Direct list paper mailing bags at 430 × 300 × 80mm **from £0.24 per bag** ([Priory Direct](https://www.priorydirect.co.uk/postal-packaging/paper-mailing-bags/paper-mailing-bags-430x300x80.html)). They are kerbside-recyclable everywhere, they take print beautifully, and they look expensive. They also fail in rain and tear at the seam under a heavyweight hoodie.

**The tax position — and why it does not decide this:**
- **Plastic Packaging Tax:** £228.82 per tonne from 1 April 2026 on packaging with under 30% recycled content, with a **10-tonne rolling 12-month registration threshold** ([HMRC via RÖDL](https://www.roedl.com/en/insights/plastic-packaging-tax-new-rates/)). A brand shipping mailers weighing ~10g each would need to ship **a million parcels** to hit 10 tonnes. Not relevant.
- **Packaging EPR:** applies at **£2m turnover AND over 50 tonnes** of packaging handled; both tests must be met ([House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10352/)). Not relevant for years.

So there is no compliance argument either way. **This is purely a brand and ethics call, and it should be made on that basis and stated honestly.**

> **DECISION: 100% recycled LDPE mailer, matte finish, `#0F0F0F`, with one 25mm OT monogram bottom-right and one line of type on the reverse:**
>
> `THIS BAG IS 100% RECYCLED LDPE. RECYCLE IT WITH SOFT PLASTICS AT ANY LARGE SUPERMARKET.`
>
> **Reasoning to give the client:** a recycled bag with a real, reachable disposal route beats a compostable bag with no route. Compostable is the option that *feels* better and performs worse, and claiming it without a route is a CMA risk. **Do not put the word "compostable" on anything.**
>
> **Revisit when:** UK councils widely accept compostable packaging kerbside, or the brand moves to a paper mailer with a poly liner for weather. Both are future decisions, not launch decisions.

**Size:** 400 × 500mm (approx. 16" × 20") with a 40mm gusset for hoodies and jeans; 300 × 400mm for tees, polos and shorts. Two SKUs, not six.

### 3.3 Sticker

- 45mm circle, matte vinyl or uncoated paper (matte — the brand has no gloss anywhere).
- `#FAF6F6` clock face with OT at 12 on `#0F0F0F`.
- **Minimum line weight in the Roman numerals: 0.5mm at 45mm diameter.** A Roman-numeral `XII` at this size will have hairlines in the original engraving-style artwork that will disappear. This artwork needs a **separate simplified cut** for small-scale use — see Section 4.5.
- *Cost: ~£30–50 for 500 at a UK sticker printer (UNVERIFIED).*

### 3.4 Thank-you card

- **100 × 150mm portrait, 450gsm uncoated**, same stock family as the swing tag.
- **Front:** one line only, in the thin wide-tracked wordmark face, centred, small, in `#0F0F0F` on natural:
  `EARN EVERY SECOND.`
  Nothing else. The temptation is to add the monogram. Resist it — the monogram is already on the mailer, the seal, the neck and the tag. The card is the quiet voice.
- **Back:** four short blocks, left-aligned:
  1. Two or three sentences from the founder, **written by him, not by a copywriter, and not reprinted every drop**. The specificity is the point — one honest line about the Black Isle, or about the first run, is worth more than a paragraph of grind copy.
  2. `WASH COLD. INSIDE OUT. LINE DRY. IT WILL FADE — THAT IS THE POINT.`
  3. `OURTIME.[TLD]` + `@[handle]`
  4. `57.5878° N  4.2383° W`
- **No discount code on the card.** A discount code on the first order teaches the customer that the price is negotiable, and this is a premium-minimal brand. If there must be an incentive, it belongs in the follow-up email (Section 7).

## 4. PRINT METHODS FOR THE GARMENTS

This is the hardest brief in the section, and there is a **structural problem in the existing artwork that will cause production failures**. It is set out at 4.5. Read that first if nothing else.

### 4.1 The four methods, for these specific artworks

**Screen print (plastisol or water-based)**
- The right method for the **large back prints**: hourglass, wordmark, orbital globe, engraved wristwatch. At 280–350mm wide, the engraving hairlines scale up into printable territory.
- **Minimum line weight: 2pt (≈0.71mm) for positive artwork, 3pt (≈1.06mm) for reverse (light-on-dark)** ([Transfer Express art guidelines](https://blog.transferexpress.com/tips-for-screen-printing-custom-artwork-tip-4-detail-guidelines/)). Some suppliers will quote 0.5pt as an absolute floor ([ooShirts](https://www.ooshirts.com/blog/how-to-prepare-artwork-for-screen-printing/)) — that floor is for smooth, light, flat-surfaced jersey. **On washed heavyweight garment-dyed cotton with a raised, irregular surface, hold 2pt as the working minimum and 3pt for anything reversed.** The whole range is reverse prints on dark bodies.
- **Mesh:** 110–160 mesh for solid coverage; **200+ for the fine engraved work** ([Printavo](https://www.printavo.com/blog/what-mesh-should-i-use-for-screen-printing/), [Lawson](https://lawsonsp.com/blogs/education-and-training/understanding-mesh-counts-thread-size-in-screen-printing/)). Ask the printer which mesh they are pulling the engraved artwork through. If the answer is 110, the detail will fill in.
- **Cost:** *screen setup £15–25 per colour per position, then £2.50–£4.00 per garment at 50 units, dropping sharply above 100 (UNVERIFIED — three quotes).* Setup cost is why screen print is wrong for a 31-colourway range unless prints are shared across colourways.

**DTG (direct-to-garment)**
- **Do not use DTG on this range.** On dark and washed garments DTG requires pretreatment, and the near-universal failure is a faint rectangular box or halo around the print, visible in daylight or after the first wash ([Impressions Magazine](https://impressionsmagazine.com/process-technique/effective-dtg-printing-on-dark-colored-garments/15974/), [Firebird Ink](https://www.firebirdink.com/blogs/news/why-is-my-pretreated-shirt-staining)). Correct application is a narrow window — roughly 16–20g per shirt, applied evenly — and softer, washed garments show the residue most.
- A pretreatment box on a `#171717` washed-black heavyweight tee is a returned garment. The aesthetic that makes this brand — washed, garment-dyed, irregular surface — is precisely the aesthetic DTG handles worst.

**DTF (direct-to-film transfer)**
- **The right method for the small chest wordmark and any fine engraved element that cannot be enlarged.** DTF prints full CMYK to a film and heat-presses it; halftones hold in the 1–2% dot range and it reproduces fine detail and gradients in a single pass ([ScreenPrinting.com](https://www.screenprinting.com/blogs/news/making-dtf-look-like-screen-printing-deep-dive-with-colin), [Printful](https://www.printful.com/blog/dtf-vs-screen-printing)).
- **The catch, and it matters for this brand:** DTF sits *on* the fabric as a film. It has a slight sheen and a distinct hand. On a premium washed garment-dyed piece, a large DTF panel will read as a sticker, and it ages differently from the fabric — the fabric fades, the transfer does not, and after six months the print looks newer than the shirt. That is fatal for a brand whose whole proposition is wear and time.
- **Rule: DTF for small marks only (under ~80mm), never for the large back prints.** Request a **matte/soft-hand DTF film** and get a sample washed ten times before committing.
- *Cost: £1.50–£3.00 per A4 transfer, plus press labour (UNVERIFIED).*

**Embroidery**
- **The right method for the OT chest mark on the polo, crewneck, zip hoodie** — heavier garments, small mark, and embroidery is the one method that survives garment washing indefinitely and reads as expensive.
- **The limits:** minimum legible text height **6.4mm (0.25")**; satin stitch width **1.5mm minimum, 10mm maximum**; fine detail must be at least **0.05" / 4pt (≈1.3mm)** ([Fourthwall](https://help.fourthwall.com/hc/en-us/articles/13331329505307-Embroidery-Best-Practices), [Wilcom](https://wilcom.com/resources/blog/mastering-basic-stitch-types-satin-stitch-and-tatami-stitch)).
- **The OT monogram embroiders well** — it is heavy and chunky, its strokes will comfortably exceed 1.5mm at a 60mm chest placement, and the angular shear cuts are achievable as satin stitch terminations. **Brief the digitiser explicitly to preserve the shear angles** — digitisers default to rounding sharp terminations because it stitches cleaner, and that would remove the mark's defining feature.
- **The OUR TIME wordmark cannot be embroidered at chest scale. Do not attempt it.** A thin sans at a 60mm chest width has stems well under 1mm, below the 1.5mm satin minimum, and would come out as a run-stitch scribble with the word space closed.
- *Cost: digitising £15–30 one-off per design, then £2–4 per garment (UNVERIFIED).*

### 4.2 Discharge printing — and why it will probably not work here

Discharge is the obvious aesthetic answer: it bleaches the dye out and replaces it, leaving a print with no hand at all, soft and vintage and sunk into the fabric. Exactly the brand.

**But discharge only works on reactive-dyed cotton.** Pigment-dyed and garment-dyed fabrics **resist discharge activation**, because the pigment sits on the fabric surface rather than bonded into the fibre, so there is nothing to strip ([Extreme Screen Prints technical guide](https://www.extremescreenprints.com/post/understanding-discharge-screen-printing-the-complete-technical-guide), [ScreenPrinting.com](https://www.screenprinting.com/blogs/news/pigment-dyeing-vs-garment-dyeing-whats-the-difference)). Overdyed garments are worse still — the original base colour shows through unpredictably.

**Practical implication:** the entire washed/garment-dyed direction of this range is fundamentally at odds with discharge printing. Before assuming it:

1. **Find out how the blanks are actually dyed** — reactive, pigment, or garment-dyed after make-up. Ask the blank supplier in writing. This is a one-email question with a large consequence.
2. If reactive: discharge is available. Specs — **156–200 mesh, minimum 1pt stroke weight, halftones at 45–55 LPI, cure at 320–330°F** (below 310°F gives incomplete discharge; above 340°F scorches). Expect batch-to-batch colour variation, and note that navy in particular needs heavy pigment loading which cancels out the softness benefit.
3. If pigment/garment-dyed: **use soft-hand water-based screen print instead.** It gets 80% of the look, it works on any base, and it is what most washed streetwear actually uses.

### 4.3 Puff

Puff is currently fashionable and it is **the wrong choice for this brand's artwork.**

Puff additive contains spherules that expand at temperature, and they expand **in all directions** — so lines thicken, gaps close, and fine detail is destroyed. General rule: **nothing under 1pt, and detail under 1–2mm will be lost entirely** ([Family Industries](https://www.familyindustries.com/blog/how-to-design-artwork-for-screen-printing-with-puff-ink), [ScreenPrinting.com](https://www.screenprinting.com/blogs/news/tips-for-printing-puff-plastisol-ink-additive-to-achieve-high-detail-designs)).

Every piece of engraved artwork in this range — hourglass, wristwatch, globe rings, Roman numerals — is fine-line. Puff would obliterate all of it. Puff also has a plasticky, raised, new-looking hand, which is the opposite of washed and worn.

> **Ruling: no puff on any OUR TIME garment.** The one defensible exception is a large solid OT monogram at 200mm+ on a single hero piece, where puff on a chunky mark reads as intentional. Even then: it fights the aesthetic. Note it in the book as a "no" with a reason, because a printer will offer it and it will sound like a good idea on the phone.

### 4.4 Method allocation across the range

| Garment | Artwork | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Oversized heavyweight tee | Large back print (hourglass / wordmark / globe / wristwatch) | **Water-based screen print**, 200+ mesh. Discharge only if the blank is reactive-dyed. |
| Tee | Small OT chest (if used) | **DTF matte** or small screen print |
| Polo | OT chest mark | **Embroidery** |
| Crewneck | OT chest mark | **Embroidery** |
| Zip hoodie | OT chest mark | **Embroidery** |
| Sweat shorts | OT mark | **Embroidery** (leg) or small screen print |
| Sweatpants | OT mark | **Embroidery** |
| Baggy jeans | Clock-face pocket print | **Screen print with pigment/discharge strike-off required** — see below |
| Baggy jeans | All-over OT pattern "in the wash" | **Not a print job at all — see 4.5** |

### 4.5 WHERE THE EXISTING ARTWORK WILL FAIL IN PRODUCTION

Four flagged failures. Each needs an artwork decision before any order is placed.

**FAILURE 1 — The wordmark at chest scale is below every method's minimum.**
The OUR TIME wordmark is a thin, light-weight sans. A light-weight sans typically has stem widths around 5–7% of cap height. At a 60mm chest placement with a ~6mm cap height, stems land at roughly **0.3–0.4mm**. Screen print minimum on washed heavyweight cotton is **0.71mm (2pt) positive, 1.06mm (3pt) reverse**. Embroidery satin minimum is **1.5mm**. The wordmark is below all of them by a factor of two to four. *(Stem-ratio figure is a typographic rule of thumb — measure the actual outlines before acting. UNVERIFIED as applied to this specific typeface.)*

> **Fixes, in order of preference:**
> 1. **Don't use the wordmark small.** The monogram is the small mark; the wordmark is the large mark. That is what the heavy/thin pair is *for*. Make it a stated rule: **the wordmark has a minimum reproduction width of 120mm on garment; below that, use the monogram.**
> 2. Where the wordmark must appear small (neck, care label), it is **woven or printed on a label**, not printed on fabric — where the substrate is smooth and the minimums are different.
> 3. If a small garment wordmark is genuinely required, **DTF matte only**, and accept the trade-offs in 4.1. Never screen, never embroidery.

**FAILURE 2 — The engraved artwork has no small-scale cut.**
The hourglass, wristwatch, globe and clock face are drawn as fine-line engravings. At 300mm on a tee back they print. At 45mm on a sticker, 26mm on a swing tag, or 20mm as a favicon, the hairlines vanish or merge into grey mud.

> **Fix: build a three-tier artwork set for every motif** and put it in the fact sheet.
> - **LARGE (over 150mm):** full engraving, all detail.
> - **MEDIUM (40–150mm):** detail reduced by roughly half, no line under 0.7mm at final size.
> - **SMALL (under 40mm):** a simplified silhouette/outline cut, no line under 1mm at final size, designed to be legible at a glance.
>
> This is normal practice for any brand with detailed marks, and it is the difference between artwork that scales and artwork that only works at one size.

**FAILURE 3 — The all-over OT pattern on the jeans is not a print job.**
An all-over tessellated pattern "in the wash" on denim is produced by **laser etching** (Jeanologia-type equipment) or by **printing the fabric before cut-and-sew**, and it is done at a **denim laundry**, not by a screen printer. A screen printer given this brief will either decline it or attempt an all-over screen, which on a made-up garment cannot cross seams, cannot reach the inside leg, and will show visible pallet edges.

> **Fix:** this piece needs a different supplier entirely, with a different lead time and a different MOQ — laundry MOQs are typically far higher than print MOQs. **Decide now whether the jeans are a launch piece or a phase-two piece.** *(Laser-etch MOQ and cost — UNVERIFIED; quote from a UK or Portuguese denim laundry.)* The realistic answer for a first drop is that the jeans move to phase two and the launch is the six knitwear/jersey styles.

**FAILURE 4 — The pocket clock-face print on washed black denim needs a strike-off before anyone commits.**
Washed black denim is typically sulphur-dyed, and sulphur blacks strip to warm brown/tan rather than to white. That could be beautiful — a sepia clock face bleeding out of black denim is exactly the brand — or it could be muddy. It is not predictable from a screen.

> **Fix: pay for a strike-off.** One pair of jeans, one screen, three ink options (discharge, white water-based, `#CFC2B4` pigment), wash tested ten times. *(£100–200 and two weeks. UNVERIFIED — but it is cheap against a ruined run.)*

### 4.6 The rule to give the printer

Put this paragraph verbatim on the supplier fact sheet:

> All OUR TIME artwork is supplied as outlined vector at 100% of final print size. No element may be scaled without approval. Minimum line weight on garment: **0.71mm positive, 1.06mm reverse.** If any element in the supplied file falls below this at the specified print size, **stop and contact us** — do not thicken lines, do not simplify, do not substitute. A physical strike-off on the actual production blank, wash-tested, is required before any run.

That last clause is the one that would have caught OVERTIME.

## 5. INSTAGRAM

Written for one person posting alone, with a job, with no photographer on retainer. Everything below is achievable on a phone.

### 5.1 The strategic point, first

**The Black Isle is the brand's biggest untapped asset, and Instagram is where it pays.**

A grind-culture streetwear label with an hourglass and "EARN EVERY SECOND" is one of several thousand. There is no way to win on that positioning — the copy is interchangeable, the motifs are interchangeable, and the audience has seen it.

**A Highland-Scottish streetwear label is one of approximately none.** Sea, firths, the dolphins at Chanonry Point, farmland, weather that changes in ten minutes, the summer light that lasts until eleven and the December days that are done by half three. That last fact is *the brand's entire thesis, physically true, in a specific place.* A place where the daylight actually runs out is where "time won't wait" stops being a slogan.

> **Interrogating it hard, as briefed:** the risk is that the Black Isle becomes tourism — heather, castles, tartan, shortbread. That version kills the brand. The Black Isle must appear as **weather, light and ground**, not as heritage. No tartan, ever. No thistles. No Gaelic type. No "Highland" in the bio. The place enters through **light quality, colour and the coordinates** — which is exactly what the existing washed, desaturated palette already looks like. The palette and the location are already the same thing; nobody has said so out loud yet.
>
> **Also interrogate this:** the brand should not claim the Black Isle unless there is a real connection to it. The coordinates were a generator artefact replaced by a deliberate choice. If the cousin has a genuine tie to that place, it is the strongest thing he owns. If he does not, the coordinates are a second fabrication and should be dropped rather than defended — this brand has already been caught once by artwork that said something untrue.

### 5.2 The ratio

**40% product · 30% location · 20% people · 10% mechanism/graphic.**

Over a rolling nine posts (what a visitor sees above the fold):

- **4 product** — flat-lay and detail. Dead-on, soft top light, near-black ground, subtle vignette, per the established direction.
- **3 location** — Black Isle. Firth, weather, light, road, sea. **No garment in frame.** These are the posts that make the grid readable and stop it being a catalogue.
- **2 people** — the garment worn, outdoors, in that light. Not studio. Not posed.
- **~1 in 10 mechanism** — a clean graphic post: the clock face, the wordmark, a coordinate, a single line of copy on `#0F0F0F`.

### 5.3 The grid rule

**Never let two flat-lays sit adjacent.** Instagram's profile grid is three across; two product shots side by side read as a Shopify feed, and the grid is the brand's shop window before any individual post is. Plan in rows of three: **product / location / product** is the strongest repeating unit, with a worn shot or a graphic breaking the pattern every few rows.

**Consistency comes from light, not from filters.** Every post — product, location, person — should look like it was shot in the same weather. That is achievable with one saved editing preset: pull saturation down, lift the blacks slightly toward `#0F0F0F` rather than crushing to `#000`, and keep whites warm rather than pure. That single preset is what makes a phone-shot grid read as a brand.

### 5.4 Post types

| Type | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Flat-lay product** | 1–2/week | Dead-on, one garment, generous negative space. Caption: product name + colourway + nothing else. |
| **Detail / macro** | 1/week | The woven label, the stitch, the print texture, the string on the tag. These outperform full-product shots and take 30 seconds to shoot. |
| **Black Isle frame** | 1/week | Shot on a walk. Caption: the coordinates, or nothing. |
| **Worn, outdoors** | 1 per 2 weeks | Needs a friend and 20 minutes of decent light. |
| **Mechanism graphic** | 1 per 2–3 weeks | One copy line on `#0F0F0F`. Rotate: `EARN EVERY SECOND.` / `TIME WON'T WAIT.` / `DISCIPLINE. FOCUS. CONSISTENCY.` / `24 / 7` |
| **Process** | occasional | Strike-offs, boxes arriving, the tags being tied. Honest, not staged. People buy from small brands because they can see the smallness. |

**Realistic cadence: three posts a week.** Not daily. A founder who commits to daily will post filler by week three, and filler is worse than a gap. Three good posts a week, forever, beats seven good posts for a fortnight.

### 5.5 Stories and Reels — where the mechanism lives

The Capaldi-era clockwork motion direction (meshing cogs, concentric rotating rings, orbiting Roman numerals, receding tunnel) **belongs in Stories and Reels, not in the grid.** The grid is still, washed and quiet; motion is the loud voice, and the loud voice should be occasional.

- **The 3-second mechanism sting.** One reusable animation: concentric rings rotating into alignment, the OT locking at 12, hold, cut. Muted greys `#A7A5A4` / `#DFDDDE` on `#0F0F0F`, no gold, no blue, no glow, no lens flare, no sci-fi. It is *clockwork*, not *time travel*. Use it as the **opening 3 seconds of every Reel and the closing frame of every drop Story sequence.** One asset, used a hundred times, becomes recognisable.
- **Drop Stories:** a 5–6 frame sequence. Mechanism sting → garment → detail → coordinate → `LIVE NOW` → link sticker.
- **Reels:** the highest-value Reel for this brand is not product — it is **a 15-second Black Isle clip with the sting at the front and the wordmark at the end.** Weather, sea, light. It travels further than any flat-lay and it takes one walk to shoot.
- **Explicitly excluded** (already killed, and they will resurface as "Reel ideas"): a scroll-scrubbed or animated hourglass draining sand; fabricated photo datelines; any always-on countdown. A countdown Story sticker on the 24 hours before an actual drop is fine — that is real scarcity. A permanent one is not.

### 5.6 The bio

Handle: secure **`@ourtime`** or the closest clean variant. **Do not take an `@overtime` handle** — the whole point of this section is that the brand has one name.

```
OUR TIME
────────────
EST. 2026 · 57.5878° N  4.2383° W
GARMENT DYED IN SMALL RUNS
EARN EVERY SECOND.
↓
[link]
```

Four lines, 150 characters. What it does: name, year, **place**, what the product actually is, and the line. What it deliberately omits: emoji, hashtags, "DM for enquiries", and any word from the grind-culture vocabulary (hustle, no days off, grind, relentless). The coordinates do more differentiating work than any adjective would.

**Link:** to the site, to THE LEDGER. Not to a link-in-bio aggregator at launch — a brand with six products does not need one, and it puts a third party's branding between the customer and the brand.

**Highlights:** three only, covers in `#0F0F0F` with the OT monogram in `#FAF6F6` — `DROPS`, `THE ISLE`, `SIZING`. Sizing as a highlight will measurably reduce returns on an oversized fit.

## 6. THE LOOKBOOK

### 6.1 Format

**Digital-first: a single PDF, 210 × 280mm portrait** (slightly shorter and wider than A4 — a proportion that reads as designed rather than as a document), **single pages, not spreads**, so it scrolls correctly on a phone. That is where it will actually be read.

**Print is phase two.** A 24pp saddle-stitched booklet on uncoated stock is the correct physical object, but at launch the money is better spent on the swing tag, which every customer touches. When it is printed: same 210 × 280mm, uncoated 120gsm text, 300gsm uncoated cover, no lamination, saddle-stitched. *(UNVERIFIED cost: £2.50–£5.00/copy at 250 copies from a UK litho printer.)*

### 6.2 Length

**20 pages.** Not 40. A first drop of six or seven styles cannot fill 40 pages without repetition, and a thin lookbook that ends while the reader still wants more is a far better outcome than a long one they abandon at page 14.

### 6.3 The sequence

The sequence is the argument. It goes **place → object → detail → line**, and it repeats. It does not go product-product-product.

| pp. | Content |
|---|---|
| **1** | Cover. `#0F0F0F` full bleed. OT monogram, 60mm, centred. Nothing else. No "Lookbook", no "SS26". |
| **2** | Full-bleed Black Isle photograph. Sea or sky. **No product.** The first thing the reader sees inside is the place. |
| **3** | The coordinates, small, in the wordmark face, on `#0F0F0F`. Vast white space. `57.5878° N  4.2383° W` |
| **4–5** | A short written statement — **80 words maximum**, set at 11pt with generous leading, in one column occupying a third of the page. This is the only place in the whole brand system where more than one sentence of prose is allowed. It should say what the brand is and where it is from. It should not use the words hustle, grind or relentless. |
| **6–13** | The range. **One garment per page**, alternating: full-garment flat-lay (dead-on, soft top light, `#0F0F0F` ground) / macro detail of that same garment — the print texture, the label, the seam. The detail pages are what make the product look expensive. |
| **14–15** | A worn spread. Outdoors, Black Isle light, walking or still, **not looking at camera.** |
| **16** | The mechanism, printed still: the clock face with OT at 12, large, silent. |
| **17** | A second Black Isle frame — weather or ground, rather than the sea from page 2. |
| **18** | The copy line, alone, on `#0F0F0F`: `TIME WON'T WAIT.` |
| **19** | Range index. Every style, every colourway, as small flat-lays in a grid with names. This is the page people screenshot and send to a friend. **Do not omit it** — it is the most functionally useful page in the book. |
| **20** | Back cover. `OUR TIME` wordmark at full width, `EST. 2026`, `OURTIME.[TLD]`, `@[handle]`, coordinates. |

**Sequencing rule to hold:** **never two product pages of the same type adjacent.** The rhythm is object / detail / object / detail. And **no prices in the lookbook** — a lookbook with prices is a catalogue, and it dates the moment prices change.

## 7. EMAIL

Three emails matter. Everything else is optional. All three are plain-text-leaning HTML — near-black `#0F0F0F` background or clean white, one image maximum, the wordmark at the top, no navigation bar, no social icon row, no stock photography.

**A structural note before the subject lines:** the brand's typographic signature is heavy/thin contrast. Email clients will not render a thin wide-tracked webfont reliably. **Set email headings in the fallback system sans with letter-spacing applied via CSS**, and accept that the wordmark in email appears as a small PNG/SVG image at the top, with alt text `OUR TIME`. Do not attempt to recreate the wordmark in live email type — it will collapse to OURTIME in half of the clients, which is the exact failure mode this brand already has.

### 7.1 Welcome — sent immediately on signup

**Subject line:** `57.5878° N, 4.2383° W`

That is the whole subject line. It is unlike anything else in an inbox, it has no verb, it sells nothing, and it is honest — it tells you where the brand is. Open rates on curiosity subject lines decay if overused; this is the one place to spend it.

*Alternative if the coordinates feel too oblique:* `Where this comes from`

**Structure:**
1. Wordmark, small, top-centre.
2. **One sentence** confirming the sign-up. `You're on the list.`
3. **Three to four short paragraphs, first person, from the founder.** Where the Black Isle is. Why the brand is called OUR TIME. What "garment dyed" means and why the colour will vary — this paragraph pre-empts the most common complaint before the customer ever orders.
4. One image: a Black Isle frame. **Not a product shot.** The welcome email is not a sales email.
5. One link: `SEE THE RANGE →`
6. Footer: coordinates, handle, unsubscribe.

**No discount code.** If there must be one, it goes in a second email 72 hours later, not here.

### 7.2 Drop — sent at launch

**Subject line:** `THE FIRST DROP. LIVE NOW.`
*Or, for subsequent drops:* `[DROP NAME]. LIVE NOW.`

Flat, declarative, uppercase, full stops. No emoji, no `🔥`, no `[URGENT]`, no `Don't miss out`. The brand's voice is short sentences ending in full stops — `OUR TIME.` `EARN EVERY SECOND.` `TIME WON'T WAIT.` — and the subject line should sound like it came from the same place.

**Preview text** (the line that shows after the subject in the inbox — most brands waste it): `[N] pieces. [N] colourways. Garment dyed in small runs.`

**Structure:**
1. Wordmark.
2. **Hero image**: one garment, the strongest piece in the drop, flat-lay on `#0F0F0F`.
3. Headline: `[DROP NAME]` + one line.
4. `SHOP NOW →` button, above the fold. `#FAF6F6` on `#0F0F0F`, square corners, wide tracking, no rounded pill.
5. A 2-column grid of the rest of the range — image, name, price. Six items maximum.
6. One Black Isle frame near the bottom, as a breath.
7. Second `SHOP NOW →`.
8. Footer.

**What is banned in this email:** a countdown timer. It was killed for a good reason and it will be offered by every email platform as a one-click block. **Scarcity that is always on isn't scarcity, it's a Shopify app.** A real deadline stated in plain text (`Ships from Monday.` / `Fifty of each.`) is stronger than a ticking GIF, because it is true.

### 7.3 Sold out / back in stock

These are two emails sharing one job: converting the miss into the next sale.

**A — SOLD OUT (sent to the list within hours of a style selling out)**

**Subject:** `[STYLE NAME] — gone.`

Three lines total:
1. `The [style name] in [colourway] sold out in [timeframe].`
2. `If you missed it, add your email and you'll be told the moment it's back. Nobody else will be.`
3. `NOTIFY ME →`

That is the whole email. Its power is entirely in its brevity — a long email about something you cannot buy is irritating. This email is also the single best list-growth mechanism the brand has, and it costs nothing.

**B — BACK IN STOCK (sent only to people who requested that specific item)**

**Subject:** `[STYLE NAME] is back.`

**Preview text:** `You asked to be told.`

**Structure:**
1. The exact item image. Not the range.
2. `You asked to be told when the [style name] in [colourway] came back. It's back.`
3. `[N] available.` — **only if that number is true.** If restocks are ongoing, say `Restocked.` and nothing more. A fabricated stock number is the same class of error as printing OVERTIME: an untrue thing on a brand surface.
4. `SHOP NOW →`
5. Footer.

**Why this email matters disproportionately:** it goes to people who have already told you they want a specific thing. It has the highest conversion rate of any email the brand will ever send, and it is the only email where genuine urgency is available — so it is the one place urgency should be used, and using it nowhere else is what keeps it working.

### 7.4 Sending rules

- **Maximum two emails a month** outside drop weeks. A brand with six products that emails weekly is a brand with nothing to say.
- **One sender name:** `OUR TIME`. Never `OUR TIME Team`, never `[Founder] from OUR TIME`, never `noreply@`. Use a real, monitored address — people reply to small brands, and those replies are the most valuable market research available.
- **Segment by one thing only at launch:** has purchased / has not purchased. That is enough. Anything more is a system to maintain that a solo founder will abandon.
- **Send drops at 19:00 UK.** *(General e-commerce convention rather than a tested figure for this audience — UNVERIFIED. Test it against 12:00 within the first three drops and keep whichever wins.)*
