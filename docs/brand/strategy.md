# BRAND STRATEGY & POSITIONING

> ⚠️ **PARTLY WITHDRAWN — see `DECISIONS.md` §1.** The Black Isle / Highland
> positioning in this section is **not** to be used. That is Oliver's decision to
> make and it has not been made. The time theme and the 24-hour index survive
> intact and never depended on the place. The argument below is kept whole so it
> can be reinstated if he ever wants it.

*OUR TIME — Brand Book, Section 01. Written September 2026.*

---

## 1. What OUR TIME actually is

### The positioning statement

> **OUR TIME is a washed, heavyweight clothing label from the Black Isle, Scotland, made for people in the unwatched middle of building something — clothes quiet enough to wear every day of the work, from a place where the length of a day is something you physically feel.**

### The argument

That sentence contains a claim that could be wrong, which is the point of it. The claim is: **the clothes should be quiet and the place should be loud.**

Right now the brand is set up the other way round. The copy is loud — EARN EVERY SECOND, TIME WON'T WAIT, DISCIPLINE. FOCUS. CONSISTENCY. — and the place is invisible (it was, until three weeks ago, Chicago, by accident). If the brand keeps that arrangement it is competing in the single most crowded, least defensible category in apparel. Motivational gym slogans are a commodity: you can buy a "Discipline Over Motivation | Gym Workout Consistency" sweatshirt on Amazon today, print-on-demand, for less than the cost of OUR TIME's blank ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Discipline-Motivation-Gym-Consistency-Sweatshirt/dp/B0GDY69RW9)), and there is a UK brand literally called [Discipline.clothing](https://discipline.clothing/) already running "consistency over motivation" as its tagline. Nothing in that space is ownable. A slogan is not a moat.

What *is* ownable: a real coastline, a real latitude, a real and extreme local experience of time, and a founder who is from there. Nobody else can print 57.5878° N, 4.2383° W and mean it.

So the strategic bet is: **OUR TIME is a place brand that happens to be about time, not a time brand that happens to have a place.** The time theme is the *expression*; the Black Isle is the *asset*. The product itself — washed, garment-dyed, muted, one chest mark, premium-minimal — is already correctly quiet. The strategy is to let it stay quiet and put the storytelling weight onto something that cannot be copied by a bedroom competitor with a Shopify theme.

**How this claim could be falsified:** if, at launch, the units that actually sell are the loud graphic back-prints to 17-year-olds who have never heard of the Black Isle and are buying the OT monogram as a sports mark, then the brand is a graphics label and the place is decoration. Watch the sell-through by SKU in the first two drops. That is the test. The recommendation in Section 3 is built so that either result is survivable.

## 2. The name's meaning, resolved

### The problem

The brand is currently saying two different things.

- **"EARN EVERY SECOND." / "TIME WON'T WAIT."** — pressure, scarcity, individual effort, the clock as an antagonist.
- **"OUR TIME."** — arrival, belonging, plural, the clock as an ally.

Held together with no explanation, those read as incoherent, and worse, they read as the default setting of every hustle-grind label since about 2016. Left unresolved, the brand drifts to the loudest of the two, because grind copy is easier to write.

### The resolution: it is an arc, and "OUR" is collective

**Confirmed — the steer is right, and there is a stronger reason for it than "it sounds nicer."**

The arc is: *you put in the hours nobody sees, and then it is our time.* The grind lines are the **first half**. "OUR TIME" is the **second half**. They are not two moods to be chosen between, they are a before and an after, and the whole brand is the sentence that joins them.

The reason this is the right call, and not just the kinder call, is that **it is already built into the logo system.** The brand owns two typographic voices and they map onto the two halves exactly:

| Voice | Mark | Half of the arc |
|---|---|---|
| **Quiet** — thin, wide-tracked OUR TIME wordmark | The waiting, the reps, the unwatched work | *Earn every second* |
| **Loud** — heavy italic OT monogram, sheared, athletic | The arrival, the team, the name on the chest | *Our time* |

That is not a coincidence to be admired, it is a rule to be enforced. **The thin wordmark carries process copy. The heavy monogram carries arrival.** Once that is stated, the "tension" in the name stops being a problem and becomes the brand's structure — every garment, every page, every animation is a move between the two. The heavy/thin contrast is not a styling preference. It is the argument.

### "OUR" is a we, not a me

Take this literally and it changes real decisions:

- Copy is written in first person plural. Never "you vs. them," never "while they sleep." The grind half of the arc is *shared* — it's a team in a cold gym at 6am, a crew in a van, a group of mates who all left the same village. It is not a lone wolf.
- Casting is **groups**, never a single hero model looking hard at the camera.
- There is no founder cult. The client is not the brand's protagonist. "OUR" makes the customers the protagonist, which is also the only version of this that scales past his mates.
- The brand's community language — members, the crew, whatever it ends up being — is a natural fit and should be built early, because "OUR TIME" is a promise of inclusion that the brand will be held to.

### Kill "OVERTIME" properly, and know why

The misprints are not a cosmetic error. **"Overtime" is the opposite brand.** Overtime is hours worked on someone else's clock, on someone else's terms, paid by someone else's hour. *Our* time is time you own. A label whose entire proposition is authorship over your own hours cannot have "OVERTIME" on its chest — the garment refutes the brand.

**Recommendation:** the OVERTIME pieces are never sold, never photographed, never listed, and never turned into a cute "misprint drop" story. A zero-month-old label cannot afford its first story to be a mistake. Give them to friends and family as unphotographed team stock, or destroy them. Then do the boring thing that prevents a repeat: one master artwork repository, one spelling, one locked letterspacing on the wordmark, and a pre-production checklist that every print file passes. That discipline is not admin — see Pillar 4, it *is* the brand.

## 3. The Black Isle question

### Recommendation: yes. Make it a Highland brand, explicitly — but as an origin, never as a costume.

This is the single highest-leverage decision in the document, so here is the full argument.

### Why yes

**1. The category is anonymous and this isn't.**
UK streetwear in 2026 is a roughly £8.6bn market growing ~4.9% year on year, and the growth has shifted from explosive to premium and sustainable ([Sourceready, UK Streetwear Market Report 2026](https://www.sourceready.com/report/detail/uk-streetwear-market-report-2026)). "Premium and sustainable" means the winners now win on story and make, not on hype velocity. In that market, an unplaced label reads as a print-on-demand shop with better photography. A placed one reads as a company.

**2. Every brand OUR TIME wants to be is a place brand.**
- **Aimé Leon Dore** is Queens, New York, and hyper-specific to one founder's actual biography — Greek-American, 90s Queens — which is precisely what made it defensible ([The Fashionography](https://thefashionography.com/fashion/menswear/the-world-of-aime-leon-dore-nostalgia-exclusivity-and-the-new-luxury/)).
- **Corteiz** is West London, started in a bedroom in 2017, and its whole mythology is geographic ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/culture/2023/03/17/the-rise-of-corteiz-inside-the-genius-marketing-strategies-of-londons-hottest-streetwear-b)).
- **Represent** is Manchester-born and still says so on a hoodie that sells globally ([Represent](https://representclo.com/collections/owners-club-hoodies)).
- **Finisterre** is the proof at the far end: founded in 2003 in a flat above a surf shop in St Agnes, Cornwall, named for the end of the earth, built on the specific problem of *cold* British water rather than California neoprene — and turning over roughly £24m by 2023 ([Field Mag](https://www.fieldmag.com/articles/cornwall-finisterre-tour), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finisterre_(retailer))).

Note what Finisterre proves: remoteness was the asset, not the handicap. The brand's identity came from the place *being* hard and far.

**3. The Black Isle gives the time theme something no competitor can fake.**
At the Black Isle's latitude, the longest day in 2026 runs **18 hours 7 minutes** and the shortest **6 hours 41 minutes** — a swing of **11 hours 26 minutes** between midsummer and midwinter ([timeanddate, Inverness](https://www.timeanddate.com/sun/uk/inverness?month=6)). That is not a metaphor about time. That is a place where the amount of daylight you are given changes by more than half a working day across the year, and everyone who lives there organises their life around it.

That single fact rescues the brand from cliché. "TIME WON'T WAIT" printed by a London label is a gym caption. The same idea, from a peninsula where winter hands you six and a half hours of light, is an observation. It also hands the brand a genuinely original, non-hacky release calendar — solstices, equinoxes, the turn of the tide — which is a far better structure than an always-on countdown timer (already correctly killed).

**4. The place has real texture, not just a name.** Chanonry Point, the spit between Fortrose and Rosemarkie, is among the best places in Europe to watch bottlenose dolphins from land, with the pod coming within yards of the shore on a rising tide, under an 1846 Stevenson lighthouse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chanonry_Point), [North Coast 500](https://www.northcoast500.com/see-do/wildlife-nature/a-highlands-hidden-gem-wildlife-watching-on-the-black-isle/)). Sea, firth, farmland, weather, low light. The existing palette — navy, washed grey, slate #54647E, cream, off-white, taupe, and the later forest green and brown — is already, whether or not it was intended, a Highland coastal palette. The brand has been half-doing this by accident.

**5. The white space is empty and sits in plain sight.**
Scottish apparel currently splits three ways, and there is nothing in the middle:
- **Heritage/luxury** — Johnstons of Elgin, Walker Slater, Eribé, Pringle of Scotland ([The Gentleman's Journal](https://www.thegentlemansjournal.com/article/scottish-style-brands-scotland-johnston-elgin-kestin-mens/), [Muddy Stilettos](https://scotland.muddystilettos.co.uk/lifestyle/fashion/cool-scottish-fashion-brands/)). Tweed, cashmere, 40+.
- **Souvenir/folklore** — whisky, thistles, Highland cows, hand-screened tourist tees ([Urban Pirate](https://urbanpirate.scot/)).
- **Urban Scottish streetwear** — Glasgow-centric and terrace/club-coded: FORTY, founded Glasgow 2013 and inspired by 90s terrace and club culture ([Silverburn](https://www.shopsilverburn.com/news/forty-pr/)); Hidden Hideout, "Glasgow born and bred" ([Hidden Hideout](https://hiddenhideout.co.uk/)).

**Nobody is doing contemporary, premium, washed streetwear from rural/coastal Highland Scotland.** That is the gap, and it is a gap with a defensible wall around it, because you cannot relocate a competitor's biography.

### Why it might be wrong — and the honest answers

| Risk | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Parochial — "who cares about a peninsula?"** | Nobody in Tokyo cares about Queens either. Specificity is what travels; genericism is what stays home. ALD's Queens-ness is why it sells globally, not despite. The rule is: be from somewhere, be *for* everyone. |
| **Limits the audience to Scotland.** | Only if the marketing targets Scotland. The Black Isle's population is a few thousand; it was never the market. It is the *origin story*, and origin stories have no geographic ceiling. Sell UK-wide from day one. |
| **Tartan cliché / shortbread-tin trap.** | Real and the biggest danger. Handled by an explicit ban list (below). The brand references the Highlands the way Finisterre references Cornwall — light, weather, sea, distance, work — never the way a gift shop does. |
| **The founder may not want it.** | Then dial it to Level 1 and revisit. But here is the test to put to him: *if you were in a shop and someone asked where the brand is from, would you say "the Black Isle" out loud without flinching?* If yes, build on it. If he hesitates, the brand isn't ready to carry it and forcing it will read as fake, which is worse than being generic. |
| **He's not "Highland enough" / it feels appropriated.** | Then don't claim heritage — claim address. "EST. 2026 · BLACK ISLE" is a statement of where the company is, which is unfalsifiable and requires no lineage. Never imply the brand is older than 2026 or has ancestral roots it doesn't have. |

### The dial: three levels, so this is reversible

**Level 1 — Origin (do this now, it costs nothing).**
Coordinates stay on the globe tee. "EST. 2026 · BLACK ISLE, SCOTLAND" appears on the neck label, the care label, the website footer, the Instagram bio, and the packing slip. No campaign, no explanation. It is simply true and stated. If the brand never goes further than this, it is still meaningfully less anonymous than 90% of its competitors.

**Level 2 — Place (recommended target within 12 months).**
- Product photography shot on location, in real Highland light — flat-lays still dead-on and near-black per the existing direction, but lifestyle and campaign imagery shot on the firth, the farmland, the point.
- Colour *names* drawn from the place (a naming convention, not a palette change — the measured hexes are locked).
- One page on the site that tells the light story honestly: 18h 07m / 6h 41m, and what that does to a year.
- Drop calendar anchored to solstice/equinox rather than arbitrary monthly drops.
- The "24 / 7" motif reframed: at this latitude, 24/7 stops being a hustle boast and becomes a description of a place where the clock and the daylight disagree violently. That is a much better joke and a much better brand.

**Level 3 — Rooted (only if Levels 1–2 land, 18 months+).**
Scottish manufacture or finishing where it's genuinely viable; a physical presence or stockist in Inverness; collaboration with a local maker; named pieces after specific places (Chanonry, Rosemarkie, Cromarty) once the brand has earned the right not to look like tourism.

**Reverse gear:** the dial only goes down cleanly if Level 1 is kept free of costume. Coordinates and an address can always be de-emphasised. A tartan capsule cannot be un-launched.

### Hard bans, permanently

Tartan. Thistles. Highland cows. Saltires. Gaelic used decoratively by non-speakers. Whisky iconography. Bagpipes. Braveheart/Outlander register. "Wee." Any typeface with a Celtic or blackletter flavour — the brand has two voices and a third would break the whole system.

## 4. Audience

### Primary: 18–26, UK, majority male, buys quality before he can really afford it

He is at the end of school, at college or university, apprenticed, in a first or second job, or training seriously at something (gym, football, boxing, music, a side business). He has £60–£120 of genuinely discretionary clothing money a month and he spends it in concentrated bursts rather than continuously. He owns few things and wants them to be correct — this is a generation that shops heavyweight-blank-first, checks the GSM, and cares more about drape and fit than about a logo.

**What else is in his wardrobe:** Nike or adidas as the base layer of everything. A Represent or Cole Buxton piece if he's saved, or the aspiration to own one. Corteiz if he's culturally plugged in. Carhartt WIP. Stüssy. Vintage or reproduction sportswear. Increasingly, unbranded heavyweight blanks. Loose-fit denim, boxy tees, baggy shorts — which is exactly what OUR TIME's range already is, and which matches what the market says is directional for 2026: heavyweight and boxy jersey, tracksuits, extra-baggy bottoms, washed finishes and refined sportswear ([Sourceready](https://www.sourceready.com/report/detail/uk-streetwear-market-report-2026)).

**Where he finds it:** TikTok first, Instagram second, and a friend third — brand discovery for this group is almost entirely social and peer-validated. He checks Vinted and Depop *before* buying at full price, which matters strategically: a brand with no resale presence reads as new and unproven, and a brand with a *collapsed* resale price reads as dead. He does not read fashion press. He may well find the brand through a person rather than an ad — a local footballer, a gym, a barber, a DJ.

**What he pays** (strategy-level bands; confirm against costing):

| Piece | Band | Reality check |
|---|---|---|
| Oversized heavyweight tee | **£45–55** | Corteiz's Royale heavyweight tee retails around $63–85 ([StockX](https://stockx.com/corteiz-royale-heavyweight-tee-navy)) |
| Polo | £60–70 | |
| Crewneck | £85–95 | |
| Zip hoodie | **£95–110** | Represent Owners Club sits near £160; Cole Buxton hoodies are £175 ([Cole Buxton](https://www.colebuxton.com/collections/hoodies)); Broken Planet £190 ([Broken Planet](https://brokenplanetsmarket.com/)) |
| Sweat shorts | £55–65 | |
| Sweatpants | £70–80 | |
| Baggy jeans | £110–130 | |

The logic: **top of the accessible band, clearly below the established grails.** An unknown 2026 label cannot charge Represent's price — Represent has had fourteen years to earn it. But underpricing is equally fatal: at £30 the tee is competing with print-on-demand and the washed, garment-dyed, heavyweight make has nowhere to show up. The £45–55 tee and the ~£100 hoodie is the band where "this is better than you expected" is a repeatable experience.

**A price is a promise.** At £50 the tee must be genuinely heavyweight (240gsm+), boxy, with a proper collar rib that survives washing, and a print that doesn't crack. If the blank can't carry the price, fix the blank before fixing the marketing.

### Secondary: 26–35, the quiet-luxury adjacent buyer

Older, more money, buys the polo and the crewneck rather than the graphic tees, wants the aesthetic without the slogan. This is the audience the Black Isle story lands hardest with, and it is why the OT-chest-mark-only pieces (polos, crews, zip hoodies) should never be treated as the boring half of the range. They are the margin and the longevity.

### Tertiary, and do not ignore it: the Highlands and Moray

Inverness and its catchment are a real, underserved, fiercely loyal local market who will back a brand that is genuinely theirs. It will not scale — but early-stage brands need a hundred people who care, not a hundred thousand who scroll past, and this is where those hundred are.

**Not the audience:** the gym-motivation buyer who wants the slogan itself. He is a large, cheap, disloyal market that would pull the brand down-market and into direct competition with print-on-demand. If "EARN EVERY SECOND" starts out-selling everything else, that's a warning sign, not a win.

## 5. Competitive set

Position the brand on two axes — **loud ↔ quiet**, and **generic ↔ placed**:

| Label | Where it sits | What OUR TIME takes / avoids |
|---|---|---|
| **Corteiz** (West London, 2017) | Loud, placed. Scarcity, no paid ads, no celebrity endorsement, gamified drops ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/culture/2023/03/17/the-rise-of-corteiz-inside-the-genius-marketing-strategies-of-londons-hottest-streetwear-b)) | **Take:** place as mythology; community over advertising. **Avoid:** copying scarcity tactics. Corteiz's drops work because demand already exceeded supply. Faking scarcity with no demand just means no sales. |
| **Represent** (Manchester, 2012) | Mid-loud, placed. 480gsm loopback, made in Portugal, ~£160 hoodie ([Represent](https://representclo.com/collections/owners-club-hoodies)) | The realistic 5-year target, and proof a non-London British city can anchor a global label. |
| **Cole Buxton** (London) | Quiet, unplaced. Luxury sportswear, vintage-athletic and Ivy references, deliberately un-loud branding, £175 hoodies ([Cole Buxton](https://www.colebuxton.com/collections/hoodies)) | **The closest aesthetic neighbour and the real competitive threat.** Almost the same visual territory. The difference OUR TIME must hold: CB is placeless and premium-priced; OUR TIME is placed and half the price. |
| **Broken Planet** (London, 2020) | Loud, unplaced. Graphic-led, monthly drops, ~£190 hoodies ([Broken Planet](https://brokenplanetsmarket.com/)) | The route not to take. Graphic novelty ages fast and has no floor. |
| **Aimé Leon Dore** (Queens, 2014) | Quiet, intensely placed | The template. Hyper-specific biography turned into intellectual property ([The Fashionography](https://thefashionography.com/fashion/menswear/the-world-of-aime-leon-dore-nostalgia-exclusivity-and-the-new-luxury/)). |
| **Finisterre** (St Agnes, Cornwall, 2003) | Quiet, intensely placed, ~£24m turnover | The British proof that a remote, weather-beaten coastal town is a commercial asset ([Field Mag](https://www.fieldmag.com/articles/cornwall-finisterre-tour)). Different category, identical lesson. |
| **FORTY, Hidden Hideout** (Glasgow) | Mid, placed — but urban Scottish, terrace/club-coded ([Silverburn](https://www.shopsilverburn.com/news/forty-pr/), [Hidden Hideout](https://hiddenhideout.co.uk/)) | The nearest Scottish competitors. Different Scotland entirely: city, football, rave. OUR TIME's Scotland is coastal, rural, light and weather. Not in conflict — potentially in alliance. |
| **Johnstons of Elgin, Walker Slater, Eribé, Pringle** | Heritage Scotland ([Gentleman's Journal](https://www.thegentlemansjournal.com/article/scottish-style-brands-scotland-johnston-elgin-kestin-mens/)) | Proof that "Scottish" already signals quality and craft in apparel — a tailwind. Age gap of 30 years means no overlap. |
| **Souvenir/folklore tees** ([Urban Pirate](https://urbanpirate.scot/)) and **motivation apparel** ([Discipline.clothing](https://discipline.clothing/), Amazon POD) | The two ditches | Left ditch: Scotland as costume. Right ditch: time as a slogan. OUR TIME's job is the road between them. |

### The white space, stated plainly

**Cole Buxton's aesthetic and make standard, at roughly half the price, with a place story none of the London labels can copy — and a Scotland that no Scottish label is currently occupying.**

The strongest one-line competitive framing for the founder: *"Everything in this price bracket looks like it came from nowhere. Ours came from somewhere, and we can prove it."*

## 6. Brand pillars

Four. Each one is only real if it changes a decision, so each has a "so what."

### Pillar 1 — Earned, not announced

The brand's default volume is low. Quality shows up in weight, wash and fit, not in logo size. The heavy OT mark is a reward for looking closely, not a billboard.

**So what:** one brand mark per garment as the rule (chest OT *or* a back-print, not both), with the all-over OT jeans wash as the deliberate exception that proves it. No accent colour is ever introduced — the most saturated thing the brand owns is a muted slate (#54647E), so *any* new accent would instantly become the loudest element in the entire identity and break the pillar on contact. When in doubt on any design decision: remove, don't add.

### Pillar 2 — Time you can feel

Not clock imagery. Not abstract urgency. Actual, local, physical time: light that stretches to 18 hours and collapses to six, tides, seasons, the particular Highland experience of a day having a *length*.

**So what:** drops are dated to real solar events rather than arbitrary monthly cadence; photography is shot in real light, not simulated; the time motifs (hourglass, clock face, orbital globe, wristwatch) are allowed to be beautiful objects but are never used to manufacture urgency. This is what makes the killed ideas stay killed — the always-on countdown, the scroll-scrubbed draining hourglass and the fabricated datelines all fail this pillar, because all three are decoration pretending to be time.

### Pillar 3 — Our, not my

The brand is a "we." The work in the first half of the arc is shared, and the arrival in the second half belongs to a group.

**So what:** campaign casting is groups, never a lone hero. Copy uses we/our/us and never addresses the customer as a competitor to other people. No founder cult, no "while they sleep" captions. Community structures — a named crew, customers in the imagery, local partnerships — get built early rather than being a "phase two" idea, because they are the literal meaning of the name.

### Pillar 4 — Consistency is the product

"DISCIPLINE. FOCUS. CONSISTENCY." is currently a slogan on a garment. It should be an operating standard the company is held to, and the OVERTIME misprints are the evidence of what happens when it isn't. A brand about earning every second cannot be sloppy about the seconds it controls.

**So what:** one master artwork repository and one source of truth for every mark, every hex, every spelling; a locked minimum letterspacing on the OUR TIME wordmark so it can never collapse to "OURTIME" again; a pre-production checklist that every print file passes before it goes to the printer; nothing ships that fails it. This pillar is the reason the rest of this brand book exists, and it is the one that most directly protects the price.

## 7. What OUR TIME is NOT

The most useful page here. Print it and keep it where decisions get made.

**On the name**
1. **Not OVERTIME.** Ever, on anything, in any typeface, as any joke. Overtime is hours on someone else's clock — the opposite of the brand.
2. **Not "OURTIME."** The word space is structural. If a supplier collapses it, the run is rejected.
3. **Not older than it is.** EST. 2026 is honest and stays honest. No implied heritage, no "since," no fabricated archive.

**On the message**
4. **Not a hustle brand.** No "rise and grind," no sleep-shaming, no 4am, no "they're sleeping while you work." The grind half of the arc is about shared, unglamorous consistency — and there's a commercial reason too: 68% of Gen Z workers say they'd take a pay cut for better work-life balance, and 42% have turned down promotions to avoid the stress ([Stacker](https://stacker.com/stories/careers/anti-hustle-culture-2026-gen-zs-rebellion-against-burnout)). Hustle-porn is a receding cultural position, not a growing one.
5. **Not a motivational-quote label.** The slogans are a supporting voice, not the product. If a design only works because of the words on it, it isn't a design.
6. **Not a lone-wolf brand.** See Pillar 3.
7. **Not the founder's personal brand.** The customer is the protagonist.

**On the look**
8. **Not neon, not loud, not maximalist.** Muted, washed, desaturated. This is already the strength; the failure mode is one panicked "we need something eye-catching" decision.
9. **No third typographic voice.** Heavy italic OT and thin wide wordmark. That's the whole system. No script, no serif, no Celtic, no blackletter, no distressed grunge display font.
10. **No accent colour.** See Pillar 1.
11. **Never sci-fi.** The clockwork/time-machine motion direction stays mechanical, muted and premium. No glowing blue, no gold-and-blue vortex, no lens flares, no time-travel VFX. The reference is *the mechanism*, not *the genre*.

**On Scotland**
12. **Not tartan, thistles, saltires, Highland cows, whisky or bagpipes.** Not ironically either.
13. **Not a tourist brand.** The Black Isle is where the company is from, not what it sells.
14. **No Gaelic used decoratively.**

**On tactics**
15. **No always-on countdown timer.** Scarcity that never ends isn't scarcity.
16. **No fabricated photography datelines**, no invented coordinates, no borrowed places. If it says where it was shot, it was shot there.
17. **No scroll-scrubbed hourglass draining sand.** The most-built cliché of every time-themed site ever made.
18. **No manufactured drop-scarcity theatre.** Corteiz's scavenger hunts work because 10,000 people already wanted the jacket. With no demand, artificial scarcity just means unsold stock and a brand that looks like it's cosplaying a bigger one.
19. **No paid influencer seeding before there's an organic audience.** Bought attention on a brand with no story converts at roughly nothing and burns cash a first-year label doesn't have.

**On the business**
20. **Not a full 31-colourway range on day one.** Seven garment types across roughly 31 colourways is a range a funded brand carries in year three. For a first drop it is a cashflow bomb, a warehouse full of slow sizes, and a photography bill before a single sale. **Recommended launch cut: ~14 SKUs** — the globe-coordinates tee (the hero), the navy hourglass tee, two wordmark tees (cream, washed black), two polos (navy, forest green), two crews (cream, navy), two zip hoodies (washed black, cream), two shorts (washed grey, washed black), the washed-black sweatpant, and the washed-black jean. Hold the rest as the second and third drops. Sell out and restock; don't launch wide and discount.
21. **Not everything to everyone.** A brand that fits every wardrobe fits no one's identity.
