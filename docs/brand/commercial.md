# COMMERCIAL ARCHITECTURE

*The business spine. Everything above this section decides what OUR TIME looks like; this section decides whether it survives its first year.*

Read this one twice. It contains the only advice in the book that can lose you money if you ignore it.

---

## 0. THE HONEST HEADLINE

Three things are true at once, and the brand has to be built knowing all three:

1. **The product is priced-and-built mid, but it looks premium.** Using premium blanks (AS Colour or equivalent) with applied print and embroidery is a completely legitimate way to start a label — Percival, a £55–£65 tee brand, is only one step further on. But it is not the same business as Cole Buxton or Represent, who cut and sew their own garments. Pretending otherwise in the pricing is how new labels die with a garage full of stock.
2. **The tee is the hero and the worst piece of business in the range.** The fleece and the polo pay the rent. Merchandising has to reflect that.
3. **The range as currently drawn — ~31 colourways across 7 types — is roughly £39,000 of stock at a thin depth.** It is not a launch range. It is a year-three range. Section 2 is the most valuable page in this book.

OUR TIME launches with zero brand equity, no press, no resale market and no stockists. It must price in the £45–£85 band. Anything above that is a bet on a reputation that does not exist yet.

**The margin rule.** A DTC clothing brand that intends to pay for customers needs roughly **2.5×–3× landed cost (60–67% gross margin)**, because that gross margin has to absorb payment fees, outbound shipping, returns, discounting and acquisition — and still leave something. At 2× you have a hobby that occasionally breaks even.

### 1.4 The bad news, stated plainly

**The tee cannot reach a healthy multiple at a credible launch price.** At £50 with two print positions it returns 2.01×. That is below standard. Three ways out, in order of sanity:

1. **Print less on the tee.** One print position, not two. Takes landed to £21.35 and the multiple at £50 to 2.34×. The back-print is the statement; a chest hit as well is a decoration tax.
2. **Buy deeper.** Print drops to £2.95/application at 100+. A trade account or distributor should also beat AS Colour's public 50+ web tier on a 250-unit order — **ask for it; the published tier is not the best price in the market.** `UNVERIFIED — depends on the account you negotiate.`
3. **Move blanks to Portugal or Turkey at scale.** At £9–£12 a heavyweight blank, the tee lands at £15–£18 and the multiple at £50 becomes 2.8–3.3×. This is the real growth path and it needs 300+ units per style. Year two, not now.

**And the tax point everybody misses:** below the VAT threshold you **cannot reclaim the 20% VAT** on blanks, print and freight. Your true cash cost is the table above **× 1.2** — a £24.85 tee costs you £29.82 in real money. Every margin figure in this section is therefore optimistic until you are VAT-registered.

### 1.5 Contribution per order — the number that decides your marketing

A single-tee order at £50:

| | |
|---|---|
| Revenue | £50.00 |
| Landed cost | −£24.85 |
| Payment fee (Shopify Basic: 2% + 25p) | −£1.25 |
| Outbound postage (Royal Mail Tracked 48, small parcel, online) | −£5.25 |
| **Contribution** | **£18.65 (37%)** |
| Less blended returns drag (see §6) | **≈ £15** |

**You cannot buy a customer for £15.** Paid social acquisition for a new fashion brand typically costs £20–£45 per first order `UNVERIFIED — brand- and market-dependent`. So: **drop one is organic only.** No Meta ads, no agency, no "growth partner". Spend the money on stock and photography instead.

A two-item order (tee + hoodie, £135) contributes roughly **£68 before returns, £55 after** — which is a real business. Everything in the store should therefore be pushing towards the second item: **free UK shipping at £75**, the hoodie as the hero of the campaign imagery, and the tee positioned as the thing you add to it rather than the thing you buy alone.

### 1.6 The jeans

There is no blank route for baggy jeans with an all-over wash pattern and a clock-face pocket print. That is bespoke cut-and-sew, and it carries: MOQ typically **120–300 pieces per style per wash**, a wash development round, a full fit-and-grading round, import duty on cotton trousers (UK Global Tariff, textiles broadly **8–12%** — check your exact 10-digit commodity code on the UK Trade Tariff), import VAT at 20% on customs value plus duty (unrecoverable while you're under the threshold), and freight.

**Realistic all-in for a first denim run: £8,000–£14,000 and four to six months** — for the single hardest garment in the range to fit, in the category with the highest return rate in apparel.

**Recommendation: cut the jeans from year one entirely.** Keep the artwork. Make them the year-two flagship, funded by the drops that come first. This is not a downgrade of the idea; it is the only order of operations in which the idea survives.

## 2. THE RANGE, RATIONALISED

### 2.1 The arithmetic nobody has done

Seven types, ~31 colourways, five sizes each:

**≈ 150 SKUs. At a bare-minimum depth of 10 units per SKU, that is 1,500 units, ≈ £39,000 of stock at cost** (more like £47,000 in cash, unrecovered VAT included). And at 10 units per SKU you sell out of M and L in a week and sit on XS and XXL forever — which is the worst of both worlds: no depth *and* no cash.

A launch label does not have a colourway problem. It has a **depth problem**. One colourway that sells through completely is worth more than five that half-sell, because the first one gives you cash and evidence and the second gives you a storage unit.

### 2.2 Drop one: make this

Three garment types. Six colourways. **320 units. ≈ £9,000 ex-VAT / ≈ £10,800 cash.**

| Piece | Colourway | Units | Landed | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oversized heavyweight tee | Navy — hourglass back-print | 70 | £24.85 | £1,740 |
| Oversized heavyweight tee | White — wordmark back-print | 70 | £24.85 | £1,740 |
| Zip hoodie | Navy — OT chest | 45 | £31.85 | £1,433 |
| Zip hoodie | Cream — OT chest | 45 | £31.85 | £1,433 |
| Crewneck | Cream — OT chest | 45 | £29.25 | £1,316 |
| Sweatpants | Washed black — OT | 45 | £28.75 | £1,294 |
| | **Total** | **320** | | **£8,956** |

Sell-out revenue: **£21,175.** Realistic 90-day sell-through of 60–70% on a first drop: **£13,000–£15,000 in, leaving £3,000–£4,000 of stock on the shelf.** Which means:

> **Drop one is designed to give you your money back and a mailing list, not a profit.** If anyone tells you otherwise they are selling you something. The profit is in drop two, when you already know which sizes sell.

**If the budget is £5,000, not £10,000:** cut to tee ×2 colourways (60 each) and zip hoodie ×2 (35 each) = 190 units, ≈ £5,200. Still a real drop. Still enough to learn from. Do not go below two garment types — a one-product brand cannot raise its AOV.

### 2.3 Why these six

- **Navy and white tees** carry the two strongest back-prints and the two safest colours in menswear. The navy hourglass tee is the brand's signature image; it should be the piece people photograph.
- **Zip hoodie in navy and cream** is the margin engine (2.67×) and the highest-AOV item you own.
- **Crewneck in cream** gives a second fleece price point at £75 for people who won't spend £85.
- **Sweatpants in washed black** because it is a set with the fleece, and because **you are launching into a British winter.**

### 2.4 Hold (make later, not now)

| Item | When | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Sweat shorts — all 6 colourways** | SS27, March | Launching shorts in November is money set on fire. Washed grey and washed black only when they come; six shorts colourways is a shop floor, not a drop. |
| **Polo — all 5 colourways** | SS27, April | Best margin in the range (2.63×) and it deserves a proper launch, not to be buried in a winter drop. Start with navy and white. Two colours. |
| **Tee: washed black, cream, orbital-globe, engraved-watch** | Drop two, Feb/Mar | These are your drop-two story. The globe tee with the Black Isle coordinates is a *campaign*, not a colourway — do not waste it as one of eight options in drop one. |
| **Baggy jeans** | Year two | See §1.6. |

### 2.5 Cut (do not make)

- **Brown and forest green polos, taupe crewneck, tan zip hoodie.** Fourth and fifth colourways in a category sell a fraction of the first two, and every one of them is another five SKUs of dead sizes. Revisit only when a colour has proven itself.
- **Navy sweat short *and* navy sweatpant *and* navy tee *and* navy polo *and* navy hoodie.** Colour discipline is a brand asset; five navy garments in a launch range is not a palette, it is indecision.
- **Any colourway you cannot photograph well.** `#171717 washed black on #0F0F0F ground is 1.07:1 — invisible.` If the product photography section can't make a colourway legible, it will not sell online no matter how good it is in the hand.

### 2.6 The rule to keep forever

> **Never add a colourway until the existing one has sold through. Never add a category until the existing one has sold through twice.**

## 3. SIZING

Sizing is where this brand will either build trust or fund Royal Mail's Christmas. UK online fashion returns run at roughly **30%, and "poor fit" is the number one reason** — with apparel benchmarks clustering in the mid-20s and 56% of apparel sellers reporting 30%+.

### 3.1 The size run

**S, M, L, XL, XXL.** Five sizes, no more, for drop one. No XS, no 3XL until demand tells you.

Size curve for an oversized/boxy fit (these skew larger than the size run implies, so buyers take their normal size or one down — the curve stays centred, it does not shift up):

| Size | Share | 70-unit tee | 45-unit fleece |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | 10% | 7 | 4 |
| M | 25% | 18 | 11 |
| L | 30% | 21 | 14 |
| XL | 23% | 16 | 11 |
| XXL | 12% | 8 | 5 |

`UNVERIFIED — this is a standard menswear streetwear curve; adjust it from your own sell-through after drop one, which is the entire point of drop one.`

### 3.2 Fit philosophy

**One block, honestly described.** The oversized heavyweight tee, the crew and the zip hoodie should read as the same body: **dropped shoulder, boxy through the chest, short-to-square length, wide sleeve.** Write it down as a sentence and put that sentence on every product page:

> *Cut oversized and boxy with a dropped shoulder. Take your usual size for the intended fit, or size down for a regular fit.*

That one line — "size down for a regular fit" — will remove more returns than any app you can buy.

### 3.3 What the size guide must contain

**Garment measurements, not body measurements. Every time.** Body measurements ("chest 38–40in") are a guess about the customer. Garment measurements are a fact about the product, and facts do not get returned.

For tops, in centimetres, per size:
- **Chest** (pit to pit, laid flat)
- **Body length** (from high point of shoulder to hem)
- **Shoulder** (seam to seam)
- **Sleeve length** (from shoulder seam)
- **Hem width**

For bottoms:
- **Waist** laid flat, **relaxed and stretched** (both — elasticated waists are the single biggest source of fit complaints)
- **Front rise**, **inseam**, **thigh**, **leg opening**

Plus, on **every** product page: **model height, model chest, and the size he is wearing.** Two models at different heights if you can afford it. State a tolerance: **±1.5cm**, because that is the truth of cut-and-sew and saying so pre-empts the complaint.

**Measure your own finished garments.** Average three pieces per size, taken *after* printing and pressing — do not republish the blank supplier's spec sheet. Print and heat press move fabric.

### 3.4 The garment-dye shrinkage disclosure

This is the entire returns bill on washed heavyweight cotton, and it must appear on the product page, not buried in an FAQ.

The mechanism, so you can explain it: garment dyeing runs the finished garment through a hot dye bath, which does most of the shrinking for you — roughly **3–5% dimensional change happens during dyeing**, leaving typically **1–3% residual shrinkage** for the customer. That is why garment-dyed pieces have character and why they still move a little.

The wording to use — on every washed or garment-dyed product, near the size chart:

> **Garment-dyed.** Each piece is dyed after it is made, so colour varies slightly between garments and no two are identical. Most shrinkage has already happened in the dye bath; expect up to a further 3% in length after the first wash. **Wash cold, inside out. Hang dry. Do not tumble dry.** Measurements are taken from unwashed garments.

Three things that sentence does: it sets the expectation, it turns a defect into a feature, and — critically — it is **evidence you disclosed the characteristic**, which matters if someone claims the garment was not as described.

Care labelling also has to be physically on the garment (see §7.5). AS Colour blanks carry **tear-out labels**. If you tear the label out to brand the neck, **you must apply your own compliant fibre-composition and care label.** Budget it; don't discover it after the print run.

## 4. THE DROP MODEL

### 4.1 The answer: both, and the split is deliberate

Neither pure model works for this brand.

- **Pure always-on** turns you into a webshop. No reason to look today rather than in March, no reason to join a list, and every piece has to earn its shelf space forever.
- **Pure drops** means your revenue is four days a year, you are permanently out of stock, and you cannot build the boring repeat business that actually compounds. It also means you are competing on hype with brands that have ten times your audience.

**The structure:**

| | **THE CORE** | **THE DROPS** |
|---|---|---|
| What | Tee (navy, white), zip hoodie (navy, cream) | The graphic stories: hourglass, orbital globe + Black Isle coordinates, engraved wristwatch, all-over OT |
| Availability | Always on, restocked | Made once, numbered, never restocked |
| Frequency | Continuous | **3 per year** |
| Size | Whatever sells | 100–250 units total |
| Why | Pays the rent, converts search and gift traffic, gives returning customers something to buy | Gives people a reason to be on the list and a reason to act today |

Three drops a year is the right number for a founder with other commitments. Four is a treadmill. Two is forgettable.

### 4.2 How a drop window works

**The window stays open until the run sells out.** Not 72 hours. Not a weekend. Not a timer.

An always-on countdown was already rejected as fake scarcity, and rightly — but the fix is not "no scarcity". The fix is **real scarcity, disclosed as a fact**:

- **State the run size on the product page.** "Run of 120." That is a number that cannot be faked and does not reset at midnight.
- **Show units remaining, truthfully**, and let it move slowly. A counter that says 87 of 120 is more persuasive than a clock, because it is information rather than pressure.
- **Leave sold-out pieces visible and marked sold out.** Do not hide them. A sold-out grid is the most credible thing a young brand owns, and it fits THE LEDGER exactly: the site is a record of what was made and what is gone, not a shop that pretends to be infinite.
- **Early access for the mailing list, 24 hours.** That is the reward for being on the list, and it is the only artificial advantage worth creating.

### 4.3 What happens to unsold stock

Write this policy down now, before you need it, because every discount decision made in a panic damages the brand permanently.

| Weeks after drop | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–8 | **Full price. No exceptions, no codes, no "10% off your first order".** |
| 8–14 | Move to an **archive / "still available"** page at **full price**, with new photography. Most of what doesn't sell in week one didn't sell because nobody saw it in the right context. |
| 14+ | Fold into bundles at full value (tee + hoodie set, small saving on the pair), or use as **gift-with-purchase** and seeding stock for people who will be photographed in it. |
| Once a year, January | **One archive sale. Maximum 30% off. One week. Announced as an archive sale, not a sale.** |
| Never | A permanent discount code, a returning-customer code, a 20% welcome popup, or a "final hours" email for stock that has been sitting for five months. |

**Never discount the core.** If the navy tee is ever on sale, the navy tee is never full price again.

### 4.4 Pre-orders

For drop two onwards, pre-orders are a legitimate way to fund the buy without risking your own capital — take orders, then place the production order. Two hard rules: **state a specific dispatch date on the product page**, and treat it as a promise. Under distance-selling rules you must deliver within 30 days unless a longer period is agreed — so agree it, in writing, on the page. A pre-order that ships late is the fastest way to turn a customer into a chargeback.

## 5. LAUNCH PLAN — FROM TODAY TO FIRST SALE

Today is **16 September 2026**. This assumes evenings and weekends, not full-time.

### Week 0 (this week) — The single source of truth
The brand has been running without one, and it has already cost you: garments exist in the world printed **OVERTIME** when the brand is **OUR TIME**.

- Create **one document** — the brand book — containing the approved spelling, the word-space rule, the price list, the colour codes and the measurement chart. Version it. Date it. Everything else is a copy.
- **Audit every artwork file.** Rename, correct or delete anything containing "OVERTIME" or "OURTIME". Do not leave a single wrong file on the drive, because the wrong file is the one the printer will open at 4pm on a Friday.
- Decide what happens to the mis-printed garments (§7.2).

**Must be true to proceed:** there is one file, and everyone working on this brand — you, the printer, the web build — is reading it.

### Weeks 1–2 (to 30 Sep) — Foundations
- **Free UK trade mark search** on the IPO register for OUR TIME and the OT device in class 25 (§7.1). Do this *before* you spend anything on labels or packaging.
- Register as a **sole trader** with HMRC (required once you earn over £1,000 in a tax year).
- Secure domain and handles. Accept that `ourtime.com` and `ourtime.co.uk` are gone (§7.1) and pick something searchable.
- Open a **trade account with the blank supplier** and ask directly what a 250-unit order costs — not the web price.
- Brief the printer/embroiderer. Send the OT mark for **digitising** (£15–£25 one-off).
- **Order samples**: one of every blank, in every colour you are seriously considering, plus one decorated sample per print.

**Must be true:** nothing on the register blocks the name, and you have a supplier who has quoted you in writing.

### Weeks 3–5 (to 21 Oct) — Samples and the wash test
- **Wash-test every sample three times**, cold, hang dried. Measure before and after. This produces your shrinkage disclosure and your size chart.
- **Fit session with three different body types.** Not just you.
- Check print durability after the washes. A back-print that cracks at wash three is a refund at week six.
- Fix and re-sample anything that failed.

**Must be true:** you are holding a garment you would personally pay £50 for, and you have written measurements for every size.

### Weeks 6–7 (to 4 Nov) — Commit
- **Place the production buy.** Deposit paid. Lead times: blanks typically 1–2 weeks, decoration 1–2 weeks after that. `UNVERIFIED — confirm with your specific suppliers and add two weeks of slack.`
- Order **woven neck labels, care/composition labels and swing tags**.
- Buy **product and public liability insurance** (§7.4).
- Write the **returns, shipping and privacy policies** (§7.6). Do this yourself now, not the night before launch.
- Build the store. **Shopify Basic: £25/month monthly or £19/month billed yearly; 2% + 25p per online UK card transaction.**

**Must be true:** you have the full cost of the buy in the bank **plus 20% contingency**, and you have not spent it on anything else.

### Weeks 8–10 (to 25 Nov) — Audience before stock arrives
- **Shoot the product** — flat-lay, dead-on, soft top light, near-black ground, per the art direction. Every colourway, every angle, plus on-body with stated model height and size.
- **Build the product pages with the real measurements on them.**
- **Seed 8–12 garments** to people who will genuinely wear them and be seen. Not influencers — people.
- **Open the waitlist.** This is the single highest-value activity in the whole plan.

**Must be true before you pick a launch date:** you have **400+ email addresses**, or 50 people who have told you personally they will buy. A launch to nobody is not a launch; it is an expensive photograph.

### Weeks 11–12 — The drop
- **24 hours early access** to the list, then public.
- Pack and post daily. Tracked 48, small parcel, £5.25 online (April 2026 rates); fleece pieces may exceed small-parcel dimensions — **weigh and measure a packed hoodie before you set your shipping rates**, or free shipping will quietly eat your hoodie margin.

**Must be true:** you can physically pack and post 40 orders across one weekend.

### The date fork — decide it on 4 November
- **On schedule on 4 Nov → launch first week of December.** Before Christmas posting deadlines, into the strongest buying window of the year.
- **Anything more than two weeks late → do not fight Christmas. Move the whole drop to 22 January.** A late December launch means Royal Mail congestion, "it didn't arrive in time" refunds and a January returns wave on your very first cohort. January is quieter and much, much kinder to a first drop.

Making that call in advance, on a date, is the difference between a founder and someone who is hoping.

## 6. THE NUMBERS THAT MATTER

Eight. That is all. Put them on one page and update it weekly.

| # | Metric | What good looks like | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Sell-through by style** at 30 / 60 / 90 days | 50% / 70% / 85% | The only honest verdict on whether you bought the right thing. Track by **size** too. |
| 2 | **Contribution margin per order** | **≥ £25** | Revenue minus landed cost, payment fee and postage. Know it per style. This, not gross margin, is what pays you. |
| 3 | **Return rate, by style and by size** | Under 25%; **UK online fashion runs ~30%, apparel benchmarks mid-20s** | A single size returning above 30% is a fit bug in your size chart, not bad luck. Fix the chart, not the garment. |
| 4 | **AOV** | **≥ £90** | UK fashion & accessories AOV was **£91.53 in May 2026**. Below £60 and you are shipping single tees at a loss on attention. |
| 5 | **Conversion rate** | **1.5–2.5%** (fashion benchmark) | Under 1% is a product-page or price problem, not a traffic problem. More traffic will not fix it. |
| 6 | **Email list size and drop-day conversion of the list** | 5–10% of an engaged list buying on drop day `UNVERIFIED` | The list is the business. Followers are not. |
| 7 | **Repeat purchase rate at 90 days** | 20%+ in year one `UNVERIFIED — treat as a directional target` | The first drop buys customers. The second drop is where you find out if you have a brand. |
| 8 | **Cash back in the bank per drop** | Drop one: break-even. Drop two: +30%. | The number that decides whether there is a drop three. |

**Stop watching:** followers, impressions, reach, likes, "engagement". None of them have ever paid a printer.

**The stock number to keep in view:** units unsold × landed cost. That is money you have already spent that is currently a pile of fabric. When it exceeds what you can afford to lose, stop buying.

## 7. LEGAL AND ADMIN

Brief, practical, and in priority order. This is not legal advice; where it matters, a solicitor costs less than the mistake.

### 7.1 Trade mark and — more urgently — searchability

**Two real problems, and the second is worse than the first.**

**Problem one: OVERTIME.** There is an established US sports-media brand, **Overtime** (Overtime Sports Inc., founded 2016), which distributes sports content and **sells apparel under its own logos and branding**. You are not that brand, and the name is not that name — but every garment printed "OVERTIME" is a garment that invites the comparison, on the exact goods they sell. `UNVERIFIED — I have not been able to confirm from the registers whether Overtime Sports holds UK or EU marks in class 25. Someone must check.`

**Problem two: you cannot be found.** `ourtime.co.uk` and `ourtime.com` belong to **OurTime, a Match Group dating service for over-50s with over 190,000 UK members**. In UK search, "our time" means that. This is not fixable by SEO effort; it is a structural fact about the name.

What to do:

1. **Run the free search** on the UK IPO register (`trademarks.ipo.gov.uk`) for "OUR TIME", "OURTIME" and "OVERTIME" in **class 25 (clothing, footwear, headgear)**. Do it before you commit to labels and packaging.
2. **File the OT device mark, not just the words.** "Our time" is a common English phrase, which makes the words alone weak and hard to register or enforce. The **heavy italic OT monogram is a distinctive device** — it is the most defensible asset the brand owns, and it is the thing to register.
3. **Costs (from 1 April 2026):** UK IPO **£205 for the first class, £60 per additional class**. Class 25 is essential; class 35 (retail services) is worth considering if you ever stock other brands. This was the first trade mark fee rise since 1998, so budget the new numbers. An attorney to do it properly typically runs a few hundred pounds on top `UNVERIFIED`.
4. **Solve search with the handle, not the name.** Pick one consistent, ownable string — the monogram plus a qualifier — and use it identically everywhere: domain, Instagram, TikTok, email. People will find you by handle and by image, not by the phrase "our time".
5. **Never use "OVERTIME" again.** Not on a garment, not in a filename, not in a caption, not as a hashtag. It is the wrong brand and it points at a bigger one.

### 7.2 The mis-printed garments

Do not sell them. Not at a discount, not as seconds, not quietly. A brand-new label whose first product in the world carries another brand's name has explained itself badly and permanently. **Destroy them, or keep them as unlisted friends-and-team pieces that never appear on the site or in a photograph.** Treat the cost as tuition for §0 and move on.

### 7.3 VAT

- The registration threshold is **£90,000**, measured on a **rolling 12-month basis** — not the tax year. You must register if taxable turnover in the **last 12 months** exceeded £90,000 (30 days from the end of that month to register), **or** if you expect to exceed it **in the next 30 days alone**.
- **Below the threshold you cannot reclaim input VAT.** Your blanks, printing, freight and Shopify fees all cost you 20% more than the invoices' net figures suggest. Every cost in §1 is really ×1.2.
- **Crossing the threshold is a cliff, not a ramp.** On a £50 tee, VAT-registration means handing HMRC £8.33 unless you raise the price. Reclaiming input VAT gives some of it back (about £5 on that tee), but not all. **Model the crossing at £75,000 and decide then whether you raise prices or absorb it.** Do not be surprised by it in month eleven.

### 7.4 Structure and insurance

- **Sole trader** is correct for now: register for Self Assessment once you earn over **£1,000** in a tax year (6 April–5 April). Move to a limited company when you are profitable, holding significant stock, or ready to hold the trade mark in a company name.
- **Product and public liability insurance: get it before the first garment ships.** Product liability is not a statutory requirement but is functionally essential under the Consumer Protection Act 1987. Typical cost for a small clothing brand is **£150–£600/year**, and basic cover in low-risk textiles can be **£50–£150**; clothing shops commonly pay under **£90/year** for £2m of public liability. **£1m minimum** cover for adult clothing.
- **Your home insurance almost certainly excludes this.** Generic home-business policies exclude product liability for manufactured goods — and probably won't cover £9,000 of stock in a spare room either. Tell your insurer the stock is there.

### 7.5 Labelling

Under the **Textile Products (Labelling and Fibre Composition) Regulations 2012** (which apply assimilated EU Regulation 1007/2011), textile products must carry **fibre composition** information, using only the permitted fibre names, on a label that is **durable, easily legible, visible, accessible and securely attached**.

Practically: the blank arrives compliant. **If you tear out the supplier's label to brand the neck, compliance leaves with it** and you must apply your own fibre-composition and care label. Get this into the production spec, not the post-mortem.

### 7.6 Returns — what UK law actually requires of you

Selling online means the **Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013** apply. These are not optional and cannot be reduced by your terms.

| Obligation | The rule |
|---|---|
| **Cooling off** | The customer may cancel within **14 days of delivery, for any reason or none** — including because they simply changed their mind. |
| **Sending it back** | Once they tell you they're cancelling, they have **14 days** to send the goods back. |
| **Your refund deadline** | Within **14 days** of receiving the goods back, or of receiving evidence they were sent — whichever is earlier. |
| **Outbound postage** | You must refund **all payments including the standard delivery charge** you originally charged. If they paid for express, you only have to refund the cheapest standard option you offer. |
| **Return postage** | The customer bears the cost of returning the goods **only if you told them so before the purchase**. If you didn't tell them, **you pay.** So say it — clearly, in the returns policy *and* visible at checkout. |
| **Worn or damaged returns** | If the value is diminished by handling **beyond what is necessary to establish the nature, characteristics and functioning of the goods**, you may deduct that amount. The legal test is explicitly *"beyond the sort of handling that might reasonably be allowed in a shop"* — try it on, yes; wear it out, no. Photograph anything you deduct for. |

Separately, the **Consumer Rights Act 2015** covers faulty goods — a short-term right to reject within 30 days — which is a different thing from change-of-mind cancellation. Your policy should address both.

**Do not write:** "no returns on sale items", "no returns without original packaging", "returns within 7 days", or "store credit only". For distance cancellations, all four are unenforceable and all four will get you a chargeback and a public argument.

**Do write** a plain-English policy, and then compete on being *better* than the minimum where it's cheap: a printed returns slip in the box, a 30-day window rather than 14, and a size chart good enough that people don't need it.
